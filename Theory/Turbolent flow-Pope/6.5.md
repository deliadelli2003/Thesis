## 6.5 Velocity spectra

Velocity spectra describe how turbulent kinetic energy is distributed among eddies of different sizes.

The two-point correlation describes turbulence in physical space, while the velocity spectrum describes the same turbulence in wavenumber space. The two descriptions are connected by a Fourier transform:

```math
R_{ij}(\mathbf r)
\quad
\longleftrightarrow
\quad
\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\kappa})
```

Here:

- the two-point correlation measures how velocity fluctuations are related at two positions;
- the velocity-spectrum tensor shows how these fluctuations are distributed among different wavenumbers.

The wavenumber is related to the physical size of an eddy by:

```math
\ell=\frac{2\pi}{\kappa}
```

Therefore:

```math
\text{small wavenumber}
\longrightarrow
\text{large eddy}
```

```math
\text{large wavenumber}
\longrightarrow
\text{small eddy}
```

### Velocity-spectrum tensor

The velocity-spectrum tensor is the Fourier transform of the two-point correlation:

```math
\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\kappa})
=
\frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}
\int
R_{ij}(\mathbf r)
e^{-i\boldsymbol{\kappa}\cdot\mathbf r}
\,d\mathbf r
```

It represents the contribution of each wavenumber to the Reynolds stresses.

The total Reynolds stress is obtained by integrating over all wavenumbers:

```math
\langle u_i u_j\rangle
=
\int
\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\kappa})
\,d\boldsymbol{\kappa}
```

The tensor contains information about:

- the directions of the velocity fluctuations;
- the direction of the turbulent modes;
- the sizes of the eddies.

It is therefore a complete but relatively complicated description of turbulence.

### Energy-spectrum function

A simpler description is provided by the energy-spectrum function:

```math
E(\kappa)
```

It removes directional information and describes only how turbulent kinetic energy is distributed among different eddy sizes.

The total turbulent kinetic energy is:

```math
k
=
\int_0^\infty
E(\kappa)\,d\kappa
```

Thus, the area under the energy-spectrum curve equals the total turbulent kinetic energy.

The dissipation rate is:

```math
\varepsilon
=
\int_0^\infty
2\nu\kappa^2E(\kappa)\,d\kappa
```

The factor involving the square of the wavenumber gives greater importance to high wavenumbers. Therefore:

- large eddies contain most of the turbulent kinetic energy;
- small eddies are responsible for most of the viscous dissipation.

In isotropic turbulence, the energy-spectrum function contains enough information to determine the complete velocity-spectrum tensor.

### Taylor’s frozen-turbulence hypothesis

Measuring the complete spatial velocity field experimentally is difficult. Usually, a stationary probe measures velocity as a function of time at only one position.

Taylor’s hypothesis assumes that turbulent eddies are carried past the probe by the mean flow without changing significantly during the measurement.

```math
\text{time separation}
\longrightarrow
\text{spatial separation}
```

If the mean velocity is in the first coordinate direction, the conversion is:

```math
r_1
=
\langle U_1\rangle s
```

where the time separation is represented by \(s\), and the corresponding spatial separation is represented by \(r_1\).

This approximation is also called the **frozen-turbulence hypothesis** because the turbulent structures are treated as approximately frozen while being transported past the probe.

It works best when the turbulent velocity fluctuations are much smaller than the mean velocity:

```math
\frac{u'}{\langle U_1\rangle}
\ll
1
```

It may become inaccurate when the turbulent structures change significantly while passing the probe.

### One-dimensional spectra

Experimental measurements commonly produce a one-dimensional spectrum:

```math
E_{ij}(\kappa_1)
```

It describes the distribution of velocity fluctuations along one measurement direction.

For example, the longitudinal spectrum is related to the longitudinal two-point correlation by a Fourier transform:

```math
E_{11}(\kappa_1)
=
\frac{2}{\pi}
\langle u_1^2\rangle
\int_0^\infty
f(r_1)\cos(\kappa_1r_1)
\,dr_1
```

Its integral gives the velocity variance:

```math
\langle u_1^2\rangle
=
\int_0^\infty
E_{11}(\kappa_1)
\,d\kappa_1
```

A one-dimensional spectrum is easier to measure than the complete three-dimensional spectrum, but it contains less directional information.

Each value of the one-dimensional wavenumber receives contributions from several three-dimensional modes. Therefore, it does not correspond to only one exact eddy size.

For isotropic turbulence, the one-dimensional spectra and the full energy spectrum are mathematically related. Consequently, measurements from a single probe can be used to estimate the distribution of turbulent energy among different scales.

### Core concept

The main relationships are:

```math
\text{two-point correlation}
\xleftrightarrow{\text{Fourier transform}}
\text{velocity spectrum}
```

```math
\text{small wavenumber}
\longrightarrow
\text{large, energy-containing eddies}
```

```math
\text{large wavenumber}
\longrightarrow
\text{small, dissipative eddies}
```

The velocity spectrum therefore provides a compact description of how turbulent energy is divided among the different spatial scales of the flow.
