## 4.2 Reynolds stresses

The Reynolds stresses

```math
\langle u_i u_j\rangle
```

describe how turbulent velocity fluctuations affect the mean flow. 


### Closure problem

For a three-dimensional incompressible mean flow, the main unknowns are:

- three mean-velocity components;
- the mean pressure;
- the Reynolds-stress components.

The Reynolds and continuity equations provide only four independent equations, but they contain more than four unknown quantities because the Reynolds stresses are also unknown.

Therefore, the system is **unclosed**:

```math
\text{number of unknowns}
>
\text{number of equations}
```

The mean-flow equations cannot be solved unless the Reynolds stresses are obtained from additional equations, experimental data or turbulence models. This is known as the **turbulence closure problem**.

### Tensor properties

The Reynolds stresses form a second-order symmetric tensor:

```math
\langle u_i u_j\rangle
=
\langle u_j u_i\rangle
```

In three dimensions:

```math
\begin{bmatrix}
\langle u_1^2\rangle &
\langle u_1u_2\rangle &
\langle u_1u_3\rangle \\

\langle u_1u_2\rangle &
\langle u_2^2\rangle &
\langle u_2u_3\rangle \\

\langle u_1u_3\rangle &
\langle u_2u_3\rangle &
\langle u_3^2\rangle
\end{bmatrix}
```

The diagonal components

```math
\langle u_1^2\rangle,
\qquad
\langle u_2^2\rangle,
\qquad
\langle u_3^2\rangle
```

are called **normal stresses**. They describe the intensity of the fluctuations in each coordinate direction.

The off-diagonal components, such as

```math
\langle u_1u_2\rangle
```

are called **shear stresses**. They describe the correlation between fluctuations in different directions and are responsible for turbulent momentum transfer.

Because the tensor is symmetric, it has six independent components rather than nine.

### Turbulent kinetic energy

The turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass is defined as:

```math
k
=
\frac{1}{2}\langle u_i u_i\rangle
=
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\langle u_1^2\rangle
+
\langle u_2^2\rangle
+
\langle u_3^2\rangle
\right)
```

Thus, \(k\) is half the trace of the Reynolds-stress tensor. It measures the mean kinetic energy contained in the turbulent velocity fluctuations.

Since the diagonal components are mean squares, they cannot be negative:

```math
\langle u_i^2\rangle\geq0
```

Consequently, the Reynolds-stress tensor is symmetric and positive semidefinite.

### Isotropic and anisotropic parts

The Reynolds-stress tensor can be separated into an isotropic part and an anisotropic part:

```math
\langle u_i u_j\rangle
=
\frac{2}{3}k\delta_{ij}
+
a_{ij}
```

where:

```math
a_{ij}
=
\langle u_i u_j\rangle
-
\frac{2}{3}k\delta_{ij}
```

Here:

- $\frac{2}{3}k\delta_{ij}$ is the isotropic part, equal in every direction;
- $a_{ij}$ is the anisotropic part, which describes directional differences.

The normalized anisotropy tensor is:

```math
b_{ij}
=
\frac{a_{ij}}{2k}
=
\frac{\langle u_i u_j\rangle}{2k}
-
\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}
```

Therefore:

```math
\langle u_i u_j\rangle
=
2k
\left(
\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}+b_{ij}
\right)
```

The isotropic component can be combined with the mean pressure:

```math
\frac{\partial\langle p\rangle}{\partial x_j}
+
\rho
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}
\left(
\frac{2}{3}k\delta_{ij}
\right)
=
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}
\left(
\langle p\rangle+\frac{2}{3}\rho k
\right)
```

Therefore, only the anisotropic component \(a_{ij}\) independently transports momentum. The isotropic component behaves like an additional pressure.

### Irrotational motion

Turbulent flows are normally rotational. For an irrotational fluctuating velocity field, the vorticity is zero.

In this special case, the Reynolds stresses satisfy the Corrsin–Kistler equation:

```math
\frac{\partial\langle u_i u_j\rangle}{\partial x_i}
=
\frac{\partial k}{\partial x_j}
```

The Reynolds-stress effect then behaves like an isotropic pressure gradient and can be included in a modified pressure. Therefore, Reynolds stresses produced by an irrotational fluctuation field do not change the mean velocity field.

### Flow symmetries

Symmetries in the geometry can force some Reynolds stresses to be zero.

For a statistically two-dimensional flow with symmetry in the \(x_3\) direction:

```math
\langle U_3\rangle=0,
\qquad
\langle u_1u_3\rangle=0,
\qquad
\langle u_2u_3\rangle=0
```

The Reynolds-stress tensor becomes:

```math
\begin{bmatrix}
\langle u_1^2\rangle &
\langle u_1u_2\rangle &
0 \\

\langle u_1u_2\rangle &
\langle u_2^2\rangle &
0 \\

0 &
0 &
\langle u_3^2\rangle
\end{bmatrix}
```

For a turbulent channel that is symmetric about its centreline:

- the mean cross-stream velocity $\langle U_2\rangle$ is an odd function;
- the shear stress $\langle u_1u_2\rangle$ is an odd function;
- the streamwise velocity $\langle U_1\rangle$ and normal stresses are even functions.

Thus, known flow symmetries can simplify the Reynolds-stress tensor and reduce the number of unknown components.
