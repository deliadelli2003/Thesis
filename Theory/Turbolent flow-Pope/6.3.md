## 6.3 Two-point correlation

The two-point correlation explains how turbolent energy passes from large to small scale, derived from the Navier-Stoks equations.

This quantity compares the turbulent velocity at two points separated by a distance vector:

```math
\mathbf{r}
```

So to the question 

> How similar are the turbulent velocity fluctuations at two different positions?

If the velocities are strongly correlated, the two points probably belong to the same turbulent structure. If they are weakly correlated, they behave more independently.

```math
R_{ij}(\mathbf{r},t)
=
\left\langle
u_i(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{r},t)
u_j(\mathbf{x},t)
\right\rangle
```

Here, $\mathbf{r}$ is the distance between the two points.

- High correlation: the two points move similarly and may belong to the same eddy.
- Low correlation: their motions are almost independent.

For homogeneous turbulence, the statistical properties are the same everywhere. Therefore, the correlation depends only on the separation between the points, not on their absolute position.


## 6.3 Two-point correlation

The two-point correlation measures the statistical similarity between turbulent velocity fluctuations at two different positions in a fluid.

Consider two points separated by a distance vector $\mathbf{r}$. If the points are close, they may lie within the same eddy and have similar velocity fluctuations. As their separation increases, the fluctuations generally become less related.

Therefore:

```math
\text{high correlation}
\longrightarrow
\text{similar turbulent motion}
```

```math
\text{low correlation}
\longrightarrow
\text{nearly independent turbulent motion}
```

The two-point correlation tensor is defined as:

```math
R_{ij}(\mathbf{r},t)
=
\left\langle
u_i(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{r},t)
u_j(\mathbf{x},t)
\right\rangle
```

where:

- $\mathbf{x}$ is the position of the first point;
- $\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{r}$ is the position of the second point;
- $\mathbf{r}$ is their separation;
- $u_i$ and $u_j$ are turbulent velocity fluctuations;
- the brackets indicate statistical averaging.

The velocity fluctuations are multiplied before averaging. Fluctuations with the same sign produce a positive product, while fluctuations with opposite signs produce a negative product. If their motions are unrelated, the positive and negative products approximately cancel, producing a correlation close to zero.

In homogeneous turbulence, the statistical properties are independent of absolute position. Consequently, the correlation depends only on the separation vector:

```math
R_{ij}=R_{ij}(\mathbf{r},t)
```

At zero separation, the two positions coincide:

```math
\mathbf{r}=0
```

The correlation then becomes:

```math
R_{ij}(0,t)
=
\langle u_i u_j\rangle
```

Thus, the two-point correlation reduces to the Reynolds stress when both velocity fluctuations are evaluated at the same point.

### Longitudinal and transverse correlations

Consider two points separated in the first coordinate direction:

```math
\mathbf{r}=r\mathbf{e}_1
```

The **longitudinal autocorrelation** compares velocity components parallel to the separation direction:

```math
f(r,t)
=
\frac{
\left\langle
u_1(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{e}_1,t)
u_1(\mathbf{x},t)
\right\rangle
}{
\langle u_1^2\rangle
}
```

The **transverse autocorrelation** compares velocity components perpendicular to the separation direction:

```math
g(r,t)
=
\frac{
\left\langle
u_2(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{e}_1,t)
u_2(\mathbf{x},t)
\right\rangle
}{
\langle u_2^2\rangle
}
```

Therefore:

```math
f
\longrightarrow
\text{velocity parallel to the separation}
```

```math
g
\longrightarrow
\text{velocity perpendicular to the separation}
```

At zero separation, a velocity fluctuation is perfectly correlated with itself:

```math
f(0,t)=g(0,t)=1
```

As the separation increases, the correlations generally decrease. At sufficiently large distances, the velocity fluctuations become nearly independent and the correlations approach zero.

For incompressible isotropic turbulence, the longitudinal and transverse correlations are related by:

```math
g(r,t)
=
f(r,t)
+
\frac{r}{2}
\frac{\partial f(r,t)}{\partial r}
```

Consequently, knowledge of the longitudinal autocorrelation is sufficient to determine the transverse autocorrelation and the complete two-point correlation tensor.

### Integral length scales

The longitudinal integral length scale is defined as:

```math
L_{11}(t)
=
\int_0^\infty f(r,t)\,dr
```

It corresponds to the area under the longitudinal autocorrelation curve and measures the distance over which turbulent velocity fluctuations remain significantly correlated.

The integral length scale therefore provides an estimate of the characteristic size of the large, energy-containing eddies:

```math
\text{large integral scale}
\longrightarrow
\text{large correlated turbulent structures}
```

```math
\text{small integral scale}
\longrightarrow
\text{rapid loss of spatial correlation}
```

The transverse integral length scale is:

```math
L_{22}(t)
=
\int_0^\infty g(r,t)\,dr
```

For isotropic turbulence:

```math
L_{22}
=
\frac{1}{2}L_{11}
```

The two-point correlation therefore connects the similarity of turbulent velocities at different positions with the characteristic spatial size of turbulent eddies.

Its Fourier transform is the energy spectrum. The two-point correlation describes turbulence in physical space, while the energy spectrum describes the same statistical information in wavenumber space.
