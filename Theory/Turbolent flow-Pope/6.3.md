## 6.3 Two-point correlation

The two-point correlation explains how turbolent energy passes from large to small scale, deriving the Navier-Stoks equations.

This quantity compares the turbulent velocity at two points separated by a distance vector:

```math
\mathbf{r}
```

So to the question 

> How similar are the turbulent velocity fluctuations at two different positions?

If the velocities are strongly correlated, the two points probably belong to the same turbulent structure. If they are weakly correlated, they behave more independently.

### Two-point correlation tensor

For homogeneous turbulence with zero mean velocity, the two-point correlation is:

```math
R_{ij}(\mathbf{r},t)
=
\left\langle
u_i(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{r},t)
u_j(\mathbf{x},t)
\right\rangle
```

Here:

- $\mathbf{x}$ is the first position;
- $\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{r}$ is the second position;
- $\mathbf{r}$ is the separation between the points;
- $u_i$ and $u_j$ are velocity fluctuations;
- the average is taken over many turbulent realizations.

**Homogeneous turbulence** means that the statistical properties are the same at every position. Therefore, the correlation depends on the separation vector but not on the original position:

```math
R_{ij}=R_{ij}(\mathbf{r},t)
```

At zero separation, the two points coincide:

```math
\mathbf{r}=0
```

Therefore:

```math
R_{ij}(0,t)
=
\langle u_i u_j\rangle
=
u'^2\delta_{ij}
```

This means that the two-point correlation becomes the Reynolds-stress tensor when both velocities are evaluated at the same point.

For isotropic turbulence:

```math
\langle u_1^2\rangle
=
\langle u_2^2\rangle
=
\langle u_3^2\rangle
=
u'^2
```

The symbol $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta:

```math
\delta_{ij}
=
\begin{cases}
1, & i=j \\
0, & i\neq j
\end{cases}
```

It indicates that different velocity components are uncorrelated in homogeneous isotropic turbulence.

### Turbulent kinetic-energy decay

For homogeneous isotropic turbulence without energy production or spatial transport, the turbulent kinetic energy is:

```math
k(t)=\frac{3}{2}u'^2(t)
```

Its evolution is:

```math
\frac{dk}{dt}=-\varepsilon
```

The negative sign means that turbulent kinetic energy decreases with time because viscosity dissipates it into heat.

### Longitudinal and transverse correlations

In isotropic turbulence, the complete correlation tensor can be described using only two scalar functions:

```math
f(r,t)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
g(r,t)
```

The correlation tensor is:

```math
R_{ij}(\mathbf{r},t)
=
u'^2
\left[
g(r,t)\delta_{ij}
+
\left(
f(r,t)-g(r,t)
\right)
\frac{r_i r_j}{r^2}
\right]
```

Suppose that the two points are separated in the first coordinate direction:

```math
\mathbf{r}=r\mathbf{e}_1
```

The function $f$ is then the **longitudinal autocorrelation**:

```math
f(r,t)
=
\frac{
\left\langle
u_1(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{e}_1,t)
u_1(\mathbf{x},t)
\right\rangle
}{
\langle u_1^2\rangle
}
```

It compares velocity fluctuations that are parallel to the separation direction.

The function $g$ is the **transverse autocorrelation**:

```math
g(r,t)
=
\frac{
\left\langle
u_2(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{e}_1,t)
u_2(\mathbf{x},t)
\right\rangle
}{
\langle u_2^2\rangle
}
```

It compares velocity fluctuations that are perpendicular to the separation direction.

In simple terms:

- $f$ measures similarity in the direction connecting the two points;
- $g$ measures similarity perpendicular to that direction.

At zero separation, each velocity is perfectly correlated with itself:

```math
f(0,t)=g(0,t)=1
```

As the separation increases, the correlation generally decreases:

```math
r\uparrow
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f(r,t),\,g(r,t)\downarrow
```

At sufficiently large separation, the two points behave almost independently, so the correlation approaches zero.

### Connection between the two correlations

The incompressible continuity equation imposes:

```math
\frac{\partial R_{ij}}{\partial r_j}=0
```

For isotropic turbulence, this gives:

```math
g(r,t)
=
f(r,t)
+
\frac{r}{2}
\frac{\partial f(r,t)}{\partial r}
```

Therefore, $f$ and $g$ are not independent. Once the longitudinal autocorrelation is known, the transverse autocorrelation can be calculated.

This means that the complete two-point correlation tensor is determined by only one function:

```math
f(r,t)
```

### Integral length scales

The **longitudinal integral length scale** is defined as:

```math
L_{11}(t)
=
\int_0^\infty f(r,t)\,dr
```

It is the area under the longitudinal autocorrelation curve.

The integral scale measures the distance over which velocity fluctuations remain significantly correlated. It therefore represents the characteristic size of the large, energy-containing eddies.

A large value means that velocity fluctuations remain similar over a long distance. A small value means that they lose correlation quickly.

The transverse integral scale is:

```math
L_{22}(t)
=
\int_0^\infty g(r,t)\,dr
```

For isotropic turbulence:

```math
L_{22}
=
\frac{1}{2}L_{11}
```

Thus, the transverse correlation length is half the longitudinal correlation length.

The main idea is:

```math
\text{two-point correlation}
\longrightarrow
\text{velocity similarity over distance}
\longrightarrow
\text{characteristic eddy size}
```

The two-point correlation is also connected to the energy spectrum through a Fourier transform. The correlation describes turbulence in physical space, while the energy spectrum describes the same statistical information in wavenumber space.

## 6.3 Two-point correlation

The **two-point correlation** measures how similar the turbulent velocity is at two different points.

```math
R_{ij}(\mathbf{r},t)
=
\left\langle
u_i(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{r},t)
u_j(\mathbf{x},t)
\right\rangle
```

Here, $\mathbf{r}$ is the distance between the two points.

- High correlation: the two points move similarly and may belong to the same eddy.
- Low correlation: their motions are almost independent.

For homogeneous turbulence, the statistical properties are the same everywhere. Therefore, the correlation depends only on the separation between the points, not on their absolute position.

At zero separation:

```math
R_{ij}(0,t)
=
\langle u_i u_j\rangle
```

Thus, the two-point correlation becomes the Reynolds stress when the two points coincide.

For isotropic turbulence, the correlation can be described using:

- $f(r,t)$: longitudinal correlation;
- $g(r,t)$: transverse correlation.

The longitudinal correlation compares velocity fluctuations parallel to the separation direction:

```math
f(r,t)
=
\frac{
\left\langle
u_1(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{e}_1,t)
u_1(\mathbf{x},t)
\right\rangle
}{
\langle u_1^2\rangle
}
```

The transverse correlation compares fluctuations perpendicular to the separation direction:

```math
g(r,t)
=
\frac{
\left\langle
u_2(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{e}_1,t)
u_2(\mathbf{x},t)
\right\rangle
}{
\langle u_2^2\rangle
}
```

At the same point, the correlation is maximum:

```math
f(0,t)=g(0,t)=1
```

As the distance increases, the correlation normally decreases toward zero.

For incompressible isotropic turbulence, the two functions are connected:

```math
g(r,t)
=
f(r,t)
+
\frac{r}{2}
\frac{\partial f(r,t)}{\partial r}
```

Therefore, knowing the longitudinal correlation is sufficient to determine the complete correlation tensor.

### Integral length scale

The longitudinal integral length scale is:

```math
L_{11}
=
\int_0^\infty f(r,t)\,dr
```

It is the area under the correlation curve and represents the approximate size of the large eddies.

- Large integral scale: velocity remains correlated over long distances.
- Small integral scale: velocity loses correlation quickly.

The transverse integral scale is:

```math
L_{22}
=
\int_0^\infty g(r,t)\,dr
```

For isotropic turbulence:

```math
L_{22}
=
\frac{1}{2}L_{11}
```

The main idea is:

```math
\text{two-point correlation}
\longrightarrow
\text{similarity between two positions}
\longrightarrow
\text{characteristic eddy size}
```

The two-point correlation describes turbulence in physical space. Its Fourier transform is the energy spectrum, which describes the same turbulence in wavenumber space.
