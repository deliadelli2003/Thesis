## 6.1 Energy cascade and Kolmogorov hypotheses

At high Reynolds numbers, turbulence contains eddies of many different sizes.

An eddy of size $\ell$ has a characteristic velocity and timescale:

```math
u(\ell),
\qquad
\tau(\ell)=\frac{\ell}{u(\ell)}
```

Large eddies can contain many smaller eddies.

### 6.1.1 Energy cascade

The largest eddies have approximately the same length and velocity scales as the overall flow:

```math
\ell_0\sim L,
\qquad
u_0\sim U
```

Their Reynolds number is:

```math
Re_0=\frac{u_0\ell_0}{\nu}
```

At high Reynolds numbers, viscosity has little direct effect on these large eddies.

The large eddies are unstable and transfer their energy to smaller eddies. These transfer energy to even smaller eddies:

```math
\text{large eddies}
\longrightarrow
\text{smaller eddies}
\longrightarrow
\text{dissipative eddies}
```

This process is called the **energy cascade**.

At the smallest scales, the eddy Reynolds number becomes small:

```math
Re(\ell)=\frac{u(\ell)\ell}{\nu}
```

Viscosity then becomes important and converts turbulent kinetic energy into heat.

The timescale of the largest eddies is:

```math
\tau_0=\frac{\ell_0}{u_0}
```

The dissipation rate scales as:

```math
\varepsilon
\sim
\frac{u_0^2}{\tau_0}
=
\frac{u_0^3}{\ell_0}
```

Thus, at high Reynolds numbers, the dissipation rate is controlled by the energy supplied by the largest eddies and is approximately independent of viscosity.

### 6.1.2 Kolmogorov hypotheses

Large eddies are affected by the geometry and boundaries of the flow, so they are generally anisotropic. As energy moves toward smaller scales, this directional information is gradually lost.

#### Local-isotropy hypothesis

At sufficiently high Reynolds numbers, the smallest turbulent motions are statistically isotropic:

```math
\ell\ll\ell_0
```

This means that their statistical properties are approximately the same in every direction.

#### First similarity hypothesis

The small-scale statistics are universal and depend only on:

- kinematic viscosity;
- dissipation rate.

```math
\nu,
\qquad
\varepsilon
```

These two quantities define the Kolmogorov scales:

```math
\eta=
\left(
\frac{\nu^3}{\varepsilon}
\right)^{1/4}
```

```math
u_\eta=(\varepsilon\nu)^{1/4}
```

```math
\tau_\eta=
\left(
\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon}
\right)^{1/2}
```

Here:

- $\eta$ is the smallest length scale;
- $u_\eta$ is the velocity scale of the smallest eddies;
- $\tau_\eta$ is their timescale.

The Reynolds number at the Kolmogorov scale is one:

```math
\frac{\eta u_\eta}{\nu}=1
```

This confirms that viscosity is important at the smallest scales.

The relationship between the smallest and largest scales is:

```math
\frac{\eta}{\ell_0}\sim Re^{-3/4}
```

```math
\frac{u_\eta}{u_0}\sim Re^{-1/4}
```

```math
\frac{\tau_\eta}{\tau_0}\sim Re^{-1/2}
```

Therefore, increasing the Reynolds number creates a larger separation between the largest and smallest turbulent scales.

#### Second similarity hypothesis

At very high Reynolds numbers, an intermediate range exists:

```math
\eta\ll\ell\ll\ell_0
```

This is called the **inertial subrange**. In this range, viscosity is negligible and the eddy behaviour depends only on the dissipation rate.

The turbulent scales can be divided into three ranges:

1. **Energy-containing range:** the largest eddies contain most of the energy.
2. **Inertial subrange:** energy is transferred from large to small eddies.
3. **Dissipation range:** viscosity converts the energy into heat.

In the inertial subrange, the characteristic velocity and timescale are:

```math
u(\ell)=(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}
```

```math
\tau(\ell)=
\left(
\frac{\ell^2}{\varepsilon}
\right)^{1/3}
```

Therefore, smaller eddies have lower characteristic velocities and shorter lifetimes.

### Constant energy transfer

Let the energy-transfer rate across eddies of size $\ell$ be:

```math
T(\ell)
```

It scales as:

```math
T(\ell)
\sim
\frac{u(\ell)^2}{\tau(\ell)}
```

Using the inertial-range expressions:

```math
\frac{u(\ell)^2}{\tau(\ell)}
=
\varepsilon
```

Therefore:

```math
T(\ell)=\varepsilon
```

The same amount of energy passes through every scale in the inertial subrange:

```math
\text{energy production}
\longrightarrow
\text{constant energy transfer}
\longrightarrow
\text{viscous dissipation}
```

### 6.1.3 Energy spectrum

The energy spectrum describes how turbulent kinetic energy is distributed among eddies of different sizes.

Eddy size and wavenumber are related by:

```math
\kappa=\frac{2\pi}{\ell}
```

Therefore:

- low wavenumber corresponds to large eddies;
- high wavenumber corresponds to small eddies.

The energy between two wavenumbers is:

```math
k_{(\kappa_a,\kappa_b)}
=
\int_{\kappa_a}^{\kappa_b}
E(\kappa)\,d\kappa
```

The dissipation produced in the same range is:

```math
\varepsilon_{(\kappa_a,\kappa_b)}
=
\int_{\kappa_a}^{\kappa_b}
2\nu\kappa^2E(\kappa)\,d\kappa
```

Because of the factor involving the square of the wavenumber, most dissipation occurs at high wavenumbers, corresponding to small eddies.

In the inertial subrange, the energy spectrum is:

```math
E(\kappa)
=
C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}
```

This is the **Kolmogorov −5/3 law**. It describes how turbulent kinetic energy decreases as the wavenumber increases.

In summary:

```math
\text{large eddies}
\longrightarrow
\text{inertial energy transfer}
\longrightarrow
\text{small dissipative eddies}
```
