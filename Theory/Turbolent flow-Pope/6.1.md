## 6.1 Energy cascade and Kolmogorov hypotheses

At high Reynolds numbers, turbulent flow contains motions of many different sizes. These motions are described as **eddies**.

An eddy is not necessarily a perfectly circular vortex. It is a region of fluid that moves in a relatively organized way for a limited time. Large eddies may contain many smaller eddies.

An eddy of size $\ell$ has a characteristic velocity and timescale:

```math
u(\ell),
\qquad
\tau(\ell)=\frac{\ell}{u(\ell)}
```

The timescale represents approximately how long the eddy takes to rotate, change significantly or break into smaller eddies.

### 6.1.1 Energy cascade

The largest eddies have approximately the same length and velocity scales as the overall flow:

```math
\ell_0\sim L,
\qquad
u_0\sim U
```

Their Reynolds number is:

```math
Re_0=\frac{u_0\ell_0}{\nu}
```

The Reynolds number compares inertial effects, which maintain fluid motion, with viscous effects, which oppose velocity differences.

At high Reynolds numbers:

```math
\text{inertial effects}
\gg
\text{viscous effects}
```

Therefore, viscosity has little direct effect on the largest eddies.

The large eddies are unstable and break into smaller eddies. These smaller eddies break into even smaller ones:

```math
\text{large eddies}
\longrightarrow
\text{smaller eddies}
\longrightarrow
\text{dissipative eddies}
```

This process is called the **energy cascade**.

Energy is transferred between eddies, but it is not immediately destroyed. It passes from the large scales to progressively smaller scales.

As the eddies become smaller, their Reynolds number decreases:

```math
Re(\ell)=\frac{u(\ell)\ell}{\nu}
```

Eventually, it becomes small enough for viscosity to dominate. Viscosity then converts turbulent kinetic energy into internal energy, or heat. This process is called **dissipation**.

The timescale of the largest eddies is:

```math
\tau_0=\frac{\ell_0}{u_0}
```

Because their kinetic energy per unit mass is of order $u_0^2$, the energy-transfer rate is:

```math
\varepsilon
\sim
\frac{u_0^2}{\tau_0}
=
\frac{u_0^3}{\ell_0}
```

The quantity $\varepsilon$ is the turbulent kinetic-energy dissipation rate per unit mass. Its units are energy per unit mass per unit time.

At high Reynolds numbers, the rate of dissipation is controlled mainly by how quickly the large eddies supply energy to the cascade. It is therefore approximately independent of viscosity, even though the final dissipation occurs through viscosity.

### 6.1.2 Kolmogorov hypotheses

Large eddies are affected by the geometry and boundaries of the flow. For example, eddies near a wall are different in the direction parallel to the wall and in the direction perpendicular to it.

Therefore, large eddies are generally **anisotropic**, meaning their statistical properties depend on direction.

As energy moves through the cascade, the small eddies gradually lose information about the original flow geometry.

#### Local-isotropy hypothesis

At sufficiently high Reynolds numbers, the smallest turbulent motions are statistically isotropic:

```math
\ell\ll\ell_0
```

**Isotropic** means that the statistical properties are the same in every direction.

The term **local isotropy** does not mean that the entire flow is isotropic. It means only that sufficiently small eddies behave approximately the same in every direction, even if the large-scale flow is anisotropic.

#### First similarity hypothesis

Kolmogorov proposed that the statistical behaviour of the small eddies is universal. This means that small eddies in different turbulent flows behave similarly when expressed using appropriate scales.

Their behaviour depends only on:

- the kinematic viscosity;
- the dissipation rate.

```math
\nu,
\qquad
\varepsilon
```

It does not depend directly on the geometry or boundary conditions of the large-scale flow.

These two quantities define the **Kolmogorov scales**:

```math
\eta=
\left(
\frac{\nu^3}{\varepsilon}
\right)^{1/4}
```

```math
u_\eta=(\varepsilon\nu)^{1/4}
```

```math
\tau_\eta=
\left(
\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon}
\right)^{1/2}
```

Their meanings are:

- $\eta$: characteristic size of the smallest eddies;
- $u_\eta$: characteristic velocity of the smallest eddies;
- $\tau_\eta$: characteristic lifetime of the smallest eddies.

The Reynolds number based on these scales is one:

```math
\frac{\eta u_\eta}{\nu}=1
```

A Reynolds number of approximately one means that inertial and viscous effects are comparable. This explains why the Kolmogorov scale marks the end of the energy cascade and the beginning of effective viscous dissipation.

The ratios between the smallest and largest scales are:

```math
\frac{\eta}{\ell_0}\sim Re^{-3/4}
```

```math
\frac{u_\eta}{u_0}\sim Re^{-1/4}
```

```math
\frac{\tau_\eta}{\tau_0}\sim Re^{-1/2}
```

As the Reynolds number increases, the smallest eddies become much smaller and faster than the largest eddies. The flow therefore contains a wider range of turbulent scales.

#### Second similarity hypothesis

At very high Reynolds numbers, there is a wide intermediate range of scales:

```math
\eta\ll\ell\ll\ell_0
```

These eddies are:

- much smaller than the energy-containing eddies;
- much larger than the dissipative eddies.

This range is called the **inertial subrange**.

The word *inertial* indicates that inertial effects dominate and viscous effects are negligible. The eddies in this range mainly transfer energy; they do not produce it and dissipate very little of it.

The turbulent scales can therefore be divided into three ranges:

1. **Energy-containing range:** the largest eddies receive energy from the mean flow and contain most of the turbulent kinetic energy.

2. **Inertial subrange:** energy is passed from larger to smaller eddies with negligible loss.

3. **Dissipation range:** the smallest eddies lose their kinetic energy through viscosity.

In the inertial subrange, eddy behaviour depends only on the dissipation rate and eddy size:

```math
u(\ell)=(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}
```

```math
\tau(\ell)=
\left(
\frac{\ell^2}{\varepsilon}
\right)^{1/3}
```

Therefore, as eddy size decreases:

- characteristic velocity decreases;
- characteristic timescale decreases;
- eddies change and break up more rapidly.

### Constant energy transfer

Let $T(\ell)$ represent the rate at which energy passes from eddies larger than $\ell$ to eddies smaller than $\ell$.

The transfer rate is approximately:

```math
T(\ell)
\sim
\frac{u(\ell)^2}{\tau(\ell)}
```

Using the inertial-range expressions:

```math
\frac{u(\ell)^2}{\tau(\ell)}
=
\varepsilon
```

Therefore:

```math
T(\ell)=\varepsilon
```

This means that the same amount of energy per unit time passes through every scale of the inertial subrange.

A useful analogy is water flowing through pipes of different sizes. If there is no leakage, the same flow rate passes through every section. Similarly, energy passes through the inertial subrange without significant loss.

```math
\text{energy production}
\longrightarrow
\text{constant energy transfer}
\longrightarrow
\text{viscous dissipation}
```

Energy is introduced at large scales, transported through intermediate scales and dissipated at small scales.

### 6.1.3 Energy spectrum

The energy spectrum describes how turbulent kinetic energy is distributed among different eddy sizes.

Instead of describing eddies directly by their size, spectral analysis uses the **wavenumber**:

```math
\kappa=\frac{2\pi}{\ell}
```

Wavenumber is inversely related to eddy size:

- small wavenumber means a large eddy;
- large wavenumber means a small eddy.

The energy spectrum is denoted by:

```math
E(\kappa)
```

A high value of the spectrum at a particular wavenumber means that eddies of the corresponding size contain a large amount of turbulent kinetic energy.

The energy between two wavenumbers is:

```math
k_{(\kappa_a,\kappa_b)}
=
\int_{\kappa_a}^{\kappa_b}
E(\kappa)\,d\kappa
```

Thus, the area under the energy-spectrum curve represents turbulent kinetic energy.

The dissipation contributed by the same range is:

```math
\varepsilon_{(\kappa_a,\kappa_b)}
=
\int_{\kappa_a}^{\kappa_b}
2\nu\kappa^2E(\kappa)\,d\kappa
```

The factor involving the square of the wavenumber strongly increases the importance of high wavenumbers. Therefore, most dissipation occurs at high wavenumbers, corresponding to the smallest eddies.

In the inertial subrange, the energy spectrum is:

```math
E(\kappa)
=
C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}
```

This is the **Kolmogorov −5/3 law**.

It means that, within the inertial subrange, energy decreases with increasing wavenumber according to a power law. Large eddies contain more energy, while smaller eddies contain progressively less energy.

The complete process can be summarized as:

```math
\text{large energy-containing eddies}
\longrightarrow
\text{inertial energy transfer}
\longrightarrow
\text{small dissipative eddies}
```

The large eddies determine how much energy enters the cascade, the inertial subrange transfers that energy and the smallest eddies dissipate it through viscosity.
