## 7 Wall flows

Most turbulent flows are influenced by solid walls. Examples include:

- flow through pipes and channels;
- flow around aircraft and ships;
- atmospheric boundary layers;
- river flows.

Near a solid wall, the mean velocity is approximately parallel to the surface. The wall slows the fluid through viscosity and produces a shear stress.

The main quantities of interest are:

- the mean velocity profile;
- the wall shear stress;
- the relative importance of viscous and turbulent momentum transport.

## 7.1 Channel flow

### Flow description

Channel flow occurs between two parallel walls separated by a distance:

```math
h=2\delta
```

The streamwise, wall-normal and spanwise directions are:

```math
x,\qquad y,\qquad z
```

The mean flow is mainly in the streamwise direction:

```math
\langle U\rangle=\langle U(y)\rangle
```

In the fully developed region, the flow no longer changes in the streamwise direction. Its statistics depend only on the distance from the wall.

The mean wall-normal velocity is zero:

```math
\langle V\rangle=0
```

The flow is also symmetric about the centreline:

```math
y=\delta
```

The velocity is zero at the walls because of the no-slip condition and reaches its maximum at the channel centre.

### Pressure and shear-stress balance

The flow is driven by a pressure decrease in the streamwise direction:

```math
\frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dx}<0
```

In a fully developed channel, there is no mean streamwise acceleration. The pressure force is therefore balanced by the shear-stress gradient:

```math
\frac{d\tau}{dy}
=
\frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dx}
```

The total shear stress is:

```math
\tau
=
\rho\nu
\frac{d\langle U\rangle}{dy}
-
\rho\langle uv\rangle
```

It contains two contributions:

```math
\text{total shear stress}
=
\text{viscous stress}
+
\text{Reynolds stress}
```

The viscous stress is:

```math
\tau_{\mathrm{visc}}
=
\rho\nu
\frac{d\langle U\rangle}{dy}
```

The turbulent or Reynolds shear stress is:

```math
\tau_{\mathrm{turb}}
=
-\rho\langle uv\rangle
```

The total shear stress changes linearly across the channel:

```math
\tau(y)
=
\tau_w
\left(
1-\frac{y}{\delta}
\right)
```

At the wall:

```math
\tau(0)=\tau_w
```

At the centreline:

```math
\tau(\delta)=0
```

The pressure gradient and wall shear stress are related by:

```math
-\frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dx}
=
\frac{\tau_w}{\delta}
```

A larger pressure drop therefore produces a larger wall shear stress.

### Near-wall shear stress

At the wall, the no-slip condition requires all velocity fluctuations to be zero:

```math
u=v=w=0
```

Consequently, the Reynolds shear stress is also zero at the wall:

```math
-\rho\langle uv\rangle=0
```

The wall shear stress is therefore entirely viscous:

```math
\tau_w
=
\rho\nu
\left.
\frac{d\langle U\rangle}{dy}
\right|_{y=0}
```

Moving away from the wall:

- the viscous contribution decreases;
- the Reynolds-stress contribution increases;
- turbulent momentum transport becomes dominant.

Thus:

```math
\text{near the wall}
\longrightarrow
\text{viscous stress dominates}
```

```math
\text{away from the wall}
\longrightarrow
\text{Reynolds stress dominates}
```

### Friction velocity and wall units

The wall shear stress defines the **friction velocity**:

```math
u_\tau
=
\sqrt{\frac{\tau_w}{\rho}}
```

Despite its name, the friction velocity is not an actual mean-flow velocity. It is a velocity scale representing the strength of the wall shear stress.

The corresponding viscous length scale is:

```math
\delta_\nu
=
\frac{\nu}{u_\tau}
```

Distance from the wall can then be written in wall units:

```math
y^+
=
\frac{y}{\delta_\nu}
=
\frac{u_\tau y}{\nu}
```

The normalized mean velocity is:

```math
u^+
=
\frac{\langle U\rangle}{u_\tau}
```

Wall units allow near-wall velocity profiles from different Reynolds numbers to be compared using the same scale.

### Near-wall regions

The value of the wall coordinate indicates whether viscosity or turbulence is more important.

#### Viscous sublayer

Very close to the wall:

```math
y^+<5
```

Viscous stress dominates and the Reynolds stress is negligible. The mean velocity is approximately linear:

```math
u^+=y^+
```

#### Viscous wall region

In the broader near-wall region:

```math
y^+<50
```

Molecular viscosity still directly affects the flow. Both viscous and turbulent stresses may be important.

#### Outer layer

Farther from the wall:

```math
y^+>50
```

The direct effect of viscosity becomes small, and momentum is transported mainly by turbulent fluctuations.

The transition can be summarized as:

```math
\text{viscous sublayer}
\longrightarrow
\text{mixed region}
\longrightarrow
\text{turbulence-dominated outer layer}
```

### Friction Reynolds number

The friction Reynolds number compares the channel half-height with the viscous length scale:

```math
Re_\tau
=
\frac{u_\tau\delta}{\nu}
=
\frac{\delta}{\delta_\nu}
```

A large friction Reynolds number means that the channel is much larger than the viscous near-wall scale.

As the Reynolds number increases, the viscous wall region occupies a smaller fraction of the total channel.

### Law of the wall

Close to the wall and at sufficiently high Reynolds numbers, the mean velocity depends mainly on:

- the wall shear stress;
- the viscosity;
- the distance from the wall.

It does not depend directly on the total channel size.

The normalized mean velocity can therefore be expressed as a universal function of the normalized wall distance:

```math
u^+
=
f_w(y^+)
```

This relationship is called the **law of the wall**.

It indicates that near-wall velocity profiles from different turbulent channel flows have approximately the same form when expressed in wall units.

### Main idea

Fully developed channel flow is driven by a pressure gradient and opposed by wall friction:

```math
\text{pressure force}
\longleftrightarrow
\text{shear-stress gradient}
```

The total stress is divided between viscosity and turbulence:

```math
\tau
=
\rho\nu
\frac{d\langle U\rangle}{dy}
-
\rho\langle uv\rangle
```

At the wall, the stress is entirely viscous. Away from the wall, the Reynolds stress becomes increasingly important.
