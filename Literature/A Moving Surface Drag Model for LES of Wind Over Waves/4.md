## 4. Model validation

The purpose of the validation is to determine whether MOSD can reproduce the main effects of moving waves on turbulent airflow.

The MOSD-based Wall-Modelled Large-Eddy Simulation is compared with:

- wave-resolved Large-Eddy Simulations;
- Direct Numerical Simulations;
- laboratory experiments.

The validation examines two main aspects:

1. average turbulence quantities, such as mean velocity and Reynolds stresses;
2. phase-dependent airflow structures produced by the moving waves.

```math
\text{validation}
=
\text{mean-flow comparison}
+
\text{phase-dependent comparison}
```

---

## 4.1 Mean velocity and Reynolds-stress profiles

### Quantities used for validation

The first comparison examines the mean streamwise velocity:

```math
\langle u\rangle
```

where:

- $\langle u\rangle$ is the average air velocity in the streamwise direction;
- $u$ is the instantaneous streamwise velocity;
- the angle brackets represent an average over time and the horizontal directions.

This is a **first-order statistic** because it contains one velocity quantity.

The comparison also examines Reynolds stresses, including:

```math
\langle u'u'\rangle
```

and:

```math
\langle u'w'\rangle
```

where:

- $u'$ is the turbulent fluctuation of the streamwise velocity;
- $w'$ is the turbulent fluctuation of the vertical velocity;
- $\langle u'u'\rangle$ is the streamwise velocity variance;
- $\langle u'w'\rangle$ is the turbulent shear stress associated with vertical transport of streamwise momentum.

These are **second-order statistics** because they contain products of two velocity fluctuations.

The velocity fluctuations are defined by:

```math
u'
=
u-\langle u\rangle
```

```math
w'
=
w-\langle w\rangle
```

The mean velocity shows the average structure of the airflow. The Reynolds stresses show the strength of the turbulence and the turbulent transport of momentum.

### Comparison with wave-resolved simulations

The mean velocity profiles predicted by MOSD generally agree well with the wave-resolved LES data.

The agreement is particularly good in the logarithmic region of the atmospheric boundary layer.

The logarithmic velocity profile can be written approximately as:

```math
\frac{\langle u\rangle}{u_*}
=
\frac{1}{\kappa}
\ln
\left(
\frac{z}{z_0}
\right)
```

where:

- $\langle u\rangle$ is the mean streamwise velocity;
- $u_*$ is the friction velocity;
- $\kappa$ is the von Kármán constant;
- $z$ is the vertical distance from the mean surface;
- $z_0$ is the effective roughness length.

MOSD reproduces the reduction in mean wind velocity caused by the wave drag.

A small overprediction of velocity appears in some cases in the upper wake region. This means that the model predicts slightly less momentum loss than the reference simulations.

In this upper region, the result is influenced more strongly by:

- the subgrid-scale turbulence model;
- the computational resolution;
- the shape of the grid cells.

Therefore, differences in this region are not caused only by the MOSD boundary condition.

### Effect of wave age

An important parameter is the wave age:

```math
c^+
=
\frac{c}{u_*}
```

where:

- $c^+$ is the dimensionless wave age;
- $c$ is the wave phase velocity;
- $u_*$ is the friction velocity.

Wave age compares the speed of the wave with the characteristic velocity of the airflow.

A small value of $c^+$ generally represents a slow or young wave. A large value represents a fast wave or swell.

For a slow wave:

```math
c
<
u
```

the air moves faster than the wave. The relative velocity is large, so the wave produces significant resistance to the airflow.

For a wave moving at a speed similar to the air:

```math
c
\approx
u
```

the relative velocity becomes smaller and the wave drag decreases.

For a sufficiently fast wave:

```math
c
>
u
```

the wave can transfer momentum to the air instead of removing momentum from it.

Therefore:

```math
\text{slow wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{wave removes momentum from air}
```

```math
\text{fast wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{wave may add momentum to air}
```

The latter situation can be interpreted as negative drag or thrust.

MOSD captures this general change in behaviour as the wave velocity increases.

### Comparison with experiments

The mean velocity profiles predicted by MOSD also agree reasonably well with the laboratory measurements.

The model reproduces:

- the approximately logarithmic mean velocity profile;
- the reduction in wind velocity caused by the waves;
- the increase in drag with wave steepness;
- the decrease in drag as the wave velocity approaches the air velocity.

For some cases, MOSD slightly overpredicts the mean air velocity. This means that it slightly underpredicts the wave drag.

However, the agreement remains good because the same MOSD formulation is used for all cases without adjusting model parameters separately for every experiment.

### Comparison with fully coupled DNS

MOSD was also compared with a Direct Numerical Simulation in which both the airflow and the water surface evolved through their interaction.

The wave used for this comparison included nonlinear corrections:

```math
\eta(x,y,t)
=
a\cos(kx-\omega t)
+
ak\,\eta_1(x,t)
+
(ak)^2\eta_2(x,t)
```

where:

- $\eta$ is the total surface elevation;
- $a\cos(kx-\omega t)$ is the principal sinusoidal wave;
- $ak$ is the wave steepness;
- $\eta_1$ is a first-order correction;
- $\eta_2$ is a second-order correction.

For this case, the agreement was less accurate. MOSD underpredicted the drag produced by the wave.

One reason is that the DNS directly resolves:

- the detailed air–water interface;
- the small turbulent scales;
- the evolution of the wave;
- the two-way interaction between the air and water.

MOSD uses a prescribed wave and one-way coupling. It therefore cannot reproduce all the physical processes contained in the fully coupled DNS.

### Fast-moving waves

MOSD was also tested for slow, moderate and fast waves having the same wave steepness.

For a moderate wave velocity, MOSD correctly predicts the reduction in drag as the wave velocity approaches the airflow velocity.

For a very fast wave, MOSD correctly predicts that the wave transfers momentum to the air.

However, it overpredicts the amount of momentum transferred. Consequently, it predicts an air velocity that is too large.

This problem may be related to the assumption that the pressure force on the leeward side is negligible.

For very fast waves, the direction of the relative airflow changes. The flow-separation assumption used for slower waves may no longer be valid.

Therefore:

```math
\text{ordinary wave conditions}
\longrightarrow
\text{generally good agreement}
```

```math
\text{extremely fast waves}
\longrightarrow
\text{momentum input may be overpredicted}
```

### Contributions of WPM and EQM

The total surface stress is:

```math
\tau^{\mathrm{MOSD}}
=
\tau^{\mathrm{WPM}}
+
\tau^{\mathrm{EQM}}
```

where:

- $\tau^{\mathrm{MOSD}}$ is the total surface stress;
- $\tau^{\mathrm{WPM}}$ is the pressure stress produced by the resolved waves;
- $\tau^{\mathrm{EQM}}$ is the frictional stress associated with viscosity and unresolved roughness.

The relative contribution of WPM depends strongly on the wave conditions.

For one steep-wave case:

```math
\frac{
\tau^{\mathrm{WPM}}
}{
\tau^{\mathrm{MOSD}}
}
\approx
0.60
```

This means that approximately 60 percent of the total surface stress was produced by the resolved-wave pressure drag.

For a less steep wave travelling faster:

```math
\frac{
\tau^{\mathrm{WPM}}
}{
\tau^{\mathrm{MOSD}}
}
\approx
0.01
```

In this case, only approximately 1 percent of the total stress was produced by the WPM.

This difference occurs because WPM stress depends strongly on both the wave steepness and the relative air–wave velocity.

### Effect of wave steepness

For small wave slopes, the WPM predicts approximately:

```math
\tau^{\mathrm{WPM}}
\sim
(ak)^2
```

where:

- $\tau^{\mathrm{WPM}}$ is the pressure stress produced by the resolved waves;
- $a$ is the wave amplitude;
- $k$ is the wavenumber;
- $ak$ is the wave steepness.

However, the validation data do not fall on one exact curve based only on wave steepness.

The wave stress also depends on:

- wave age;
- Reynolds number;
- air–wave relative velocity;
- surface conditions;
- other properties of the turbulent flow.

Therefore:

```math
\text{wave drag}
\neq
\text{function of wave steepness only}
```

A more complete interpretation is:

```math
\text{wave drag}
=
f
\left(
\text{steepness},
\text{wave age},
\text{Reynolds number},
\text{surface conditions}
\right)
```

The results show that the fraction of stress produced by wave form drag generally decreases as the wave age increases.

### Main result of Section 4.1

MOSD generally reproduces:

- mean velocity profiles;
- logarithmic-layer behaviour;
- Reynolds-stress profiles;
- the increase in drag caused by steep waves;
- the decrease in drag as wave velocity increases;
- the possibility of momentum transfer from fast waves to the air.

The largest differences occur:

- very close to the water surface;
- for fully coupled evolving waves;
- for extremely fast waves.

## 4.2 Phase-averaged and wave-induced motions

Mean velocity and Reynolds-stress profiles show the average behaviour of the airflow.

However, they do not show how the airflow changes between the windward side, crest, leeward side and trough of the wave.

Phase averaging is used to isolate these wave-dependent changes.

### Meaning of wave phase

The phase identifies a particular position within the wave cycle.

```math
\phi=0
```

and:

```math
\phi=2\pi
```

represent the same point in the cycle.

Different values of $\phi$ correspond to different positions of the wave, such as the crest, descending slope, trough and rising slope.

Phase averaging combines measurements taken at the same phase during many different wave periods.

```math
\text{same wave phase over many cycles}
\longrightarrow
\text{phase average}
```

Random turbulent fluctuations vary from one cycle to another and tend to cancel during this averaging.

The repeatable motion connected to the wave remains.

### Triple decomposition

A general flow quantity is separated into three parts:

```math
q(x,y,z,t)
=
\langle q\rangle(z)
+
\widetilde{q}(x,z,\phi)
+
q'(x,y,z,t)
```

where:

- $q$ is any instantaneous flow quantity, such as velocity or stress;
- $\langle q\rangle$ is the overall mean component;
- $\widetilde{q}$ is the organized wave-induced component;
- $q'$ is the random turbulent fluctuation;
- $\phi$ is the wave phase;
- $x$, $y$ and $z$ are the spatial coordinates;
- $t$ is time.

The decomposition can be understood as:

```math
\text{instantaneous quantity}
=
\text{overall mean}
+
\text{repeatable wave effect}
+
\text{random turbulence}
```

This differs from the ordinary Reynolds decomposition because it separates the organized wave-induced motion from the random turbulent motion.

The ordinary Reynolds decomposition contains only:

```math
q
=
\langle q\rangle
+
q'
```

The triple decomposition adds the wave-induced component:

```math
q
=
\langle q\rangle
+
\widetilde{q}
+
q'
```

### Phase-averaged quantity

The phase-averaged quantity is calculated by averaging the signal at the same phase over many wave periods:

```math
q_{\phi}(x,z,\phi)
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{n=0}^{N}
\left\langle
q
\left(
x,y,z,
\left(
\frac{\phi}{2\pi}+n
\right)T
\right)
\right\rangle_y
```

where:

- $q_{\phi}$ is the phase-averaged quantity;
- $\phi$ is the selected wave phase;
- $T$ is the wave period;
- $n$ identifies the wave cycle;
- $N$ is the number of wave cycles used in the average;
- $\langle\cdot\rangle_y$ represents averaging in the lateral direction.

The wave-induced component is:

```math
\widetilde{q}
=
q_{\phi}
-
\langle q\rangle
```

where:

- $\widetilde{q}$ is the part caused specifically by the wave;
- $q_{\phi}$ is the phase-averaged quantity;
- $\langle q\rangle$ is the overall mean.

If the wave has no organized effect on the quantity, the phase average is approximately equal to the overall average and the wave-induced component is approximately zero.

### Wave-induced velocity

The validation shows that MOSD reproduces the main pattern of the wave-induced airflow.

On the windward side:

- the horizontal airflow accelerates;
- the vertical velocity becomes positive;
- the airflow moves upward toward the crest.

On the leeward side:

- the horizontal airflow decelerates;
- the vertical velocity becomes negative;
- the airflow moves downward after the crest.

```math
\text{windward side}
\longrightarrow
\text{acceleration and upward motion}
```

```math
\text{leeward side}
\longrightarrow
\text{deceleration and downward motion}
```

This organized motion is called the **pumping effect**.

The moving wave produces alternating upward and downward airflow as each crest and trough passes.

### Origin of the vertical motion

MOSD directly applies a horizontal stress at the lower boundary. It does not directly impose a vertical velocity.

The vertical motion appears because the airflow is incompressible:

```math
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}
=
0
```

When the airflow approaches the windward side and changes horizontally, conservation of volume requires a corresponding vertical motion.

Therefore:

```math
\text{horizontal wave forcing}
+
\text{continuity}
\longrightarrow
\text{vertical airflow}
```

MOSD reproduces the general shape of this vertical motion. However, its magnitude is smaller than in the wave-resolved reference data.

The phase-averaged motions are underpredicted by approximately a factor between two and three.

This is an important limitation: MOSD captures the correct qualitative pattern but not the complete strength of the wave-induced motion.

### Wave-induced Reynolds shear stress

The turbulent shear stress specifically associated with the wave is:

```math
\widetilde{u'w'}
=
(u'w')_{\phi}
-
\langle u'w'\rangle
```

where:

- $\widetilde{u'w'}$ is the wave-induced Reynolds shear stress;
- $(u'w')_{\phi}$ is the phase-averaged turbulent shear stress;
- $\langle u'w'\rangle$ is the overall time- and horizontally averaged turbulent shear stress;
- $u'$ is the streamwise turbulent velocity fluctuation;
- $w'$ is the vertical turbulent velocity fluctuation.

The results show:

- positive wave-induced shear stress on the windward side near the surface and crest;
- negative wave-induced shear stress on the leeward side;
- an alternating positive–negative pattern following the moving waves.

This pattern extends well above the water surface:

```math
\frac{z}{h_{\mathrm{bl}}}
\approx
0.5
```

where:

- $z$ is the vertical distance from the mean water surface;
- $h_{\mathrm{bl}}$ is the boundary-layer height.

This means that the effect of the waves is not confined to the immediate surface. It can influence a significant part of the atmospheric boundary layer.

### Wave-induced momentum transfer

The wave-induced momentum flux is represented by:

```math
\widetilde{u}_1\widetilde{w}_1
```

where:

- $\widetilde{u}_1$ is the wave-induced horizontal velocity;
- $\widetilde{w}_1$ is the wave-induced vertical velocity.

The strongest wave-induced momentum transfer occurs:

- on the windward side;
- close to the wave crest.

A smaller secondary maximum appears immediately above and slightly downstream of the crest.

This supports the main WPM assumption that the large-wave form drag is concentrated on the windward side.

### Accuracy and computational cost

MOSD reproduces the overall drag reasonably well over a range of wave conditions.

It also captures the main phase-dependent airflow patterns, although their magnitude is underpredicted.

Compared with wave-resolved LES, MOSD requires a much coarser computational grid.

For one comparison case, the estimated reduction in computational cost was approximately:

```math
200
\text{ to }
2000
```

times.

The exact reduction depends on the grid resolution and time-step requirements.

### Main result of Section 4.2

MOSD can reproduce:

- acceleration over the windward side and crest;
- deceleration over the leeward side;
- upward and downward wave-induced motions;
- the pumping effect;
- the alternating pattern of wave-induced Reynolds stress;
- the concentration of momentum transfer near the windward side and crest.

However, MOSD underpredicts the magnitude of some phase-dependent motions because it applies a simplified horizontal boundary stress rather than resolving the complete airflow around the wave.

---

## Core result of the validation

The validation shows that MOSD provides a useful compromise between accuracy and computational cost.

```math
\text{MOSD}
=
\text{good mean-flow prediction}
+
\text{qualitative phase information}
+
\text{low computational cost}
```

Its main strengths are:

- good mean velocity predictions for many wave conditions;
- reasonable Reynolds-stress predictions;
- representation of wave-phase-dependent airflow;
- no need for a wave-following computational grid;
- much lower computational cost than wave-resolved simulations.

Its main weaknesses are:

- reduced accuracy extremely close to the surface;
- underprediction of phase-induced velocity amplitudes;
- underprediction of drag in a fully coupled DNS case;
- overprediction of momentum input for extremely fast waves;
- inability to calculate the evolution of the waves.

Source: [Ayala et al., *A Moving Surface Drag Model for LES of Wind Over Waves*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10546-024-00884-8)
