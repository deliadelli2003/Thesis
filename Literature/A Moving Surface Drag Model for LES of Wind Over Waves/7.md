## 7. Summary and conclusions

Accurately representing momentum transfer between the ocean and atmosphere is important for:

- atmospheric-boundary-layer modelling;
- weather and climate simulations;
- offshore wind-energy simulations;
- studies of wind–wave interactions.

Existing numerical approaches generally fall into two groups.

### Wave-phase-resolved methods

Wave-phase-resolved methods directly represent the shape of the water surface and the airflow around it.

Examples include:

- Direct Numerical Simulation;
- wave-resolved Large-Eddy Simulation;
- wave-following computational grids;
- two-fluid methods.

These methods provide detailed information about the airflow at different positions along the wave.

However, they require:

- very fine computational grids;
- small time steps;
- complex moving or deforming grids;
- high computational cost.

```math
\text{wave-resolved method}
=
\text{high detail}
+
\text{high computational cost}
```

### Wave-phase-averaged methods

Wave-phase-averaged methods do not represent individual wave crests and troughs.

Instead, the entire ocean surface is replaced by an effective roughness length:

```math
z_0
```

where $z_0$ represents the average resistance created by the water surface.

These models are inexpensive, but they cannot represent how the airflow changes between:

- the windward side;
- the wave crest;
- the leeward side;
- the wave trough.

```math
\text{phase-averaged method}
=
\text{low computational cost}
+
\text{no local wave-phase information}
```

### Position of MOSD between the two approaches

MOSD was developed as a middle ground between these approaches.

```math
\text{phase-averaged model}
\longrightarrow
\text{MOSD}
\longrightarrow
\text{fully wave-resolved model}
```

MOSD retains information about the shape, slope, position and movement of the large waves.

At the same time, it does not require the computational grid to follow the curved water surface.

```math
\text{MOSD}
=
\text{phase-dependent wave effects}
+
\text{moderate computational cost}
```

---

## Main structure of MOSD

The total surface stress is:

```math
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{MOSD}}
=
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
+
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{EQM}}
```

where:

- $\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{MOSD}}$ is the total stress applied to the airflow;
- $\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}$ is the pressure stress produced by the large resolved waves;
- $\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{EQM}}$ is the frictional stress produced by viscosity and small unresolved surface features;
- $i$ identifies one of the horizontal directions.

The two parts have different functions:

```math
\text{WPM}
\longrightarrow
\text{large resolved waves}
```

```math
\text{EQM}
\longrightarrow
\text{small unresolved waves and viscosity}
```

### Windward Potential Flow Model

The Windward Potential Flow Model calculates the pressure force acting on the windward side of the resolved waves.

It uses:

- the local air velocity;
- the local wave velocity;
- the local wave slope;
- the direction of the wave surface.

The main relative velocity is:

```math
\boldsymbol{u}
-
\boldsymbol{C}
```

where:

- $\boldsymbol{u}$ is the horizontal air velocity;
- $\boldsymbol{C}$ is the horizontal velocity of the wave surface.

The local slope direction is:

```math
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
=
\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta}
{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|}
```

where:

- $\eta$ is the water-surface elevation;
- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta$ is the horizontal surface gradient;
- $|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|$ is the local wave steepness;
- $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ is the direction in which the surface rises most rapidly.

The WPM stress mainly scales as:

```math
\tau^{\mathrm{WPM}}
\sim
(\text{relative velocity})^2
(\text{wave slope})^2
```

Therefore, steeper waves and larger relative air–wave velocities produce stronger pressure drag.

### Equilibrium Wall Model

The Equilibrium Wall Model represents:

- viscous friction;
- unresolved ripples;
- small capillary waves;
- other surface features smaller than the LES grid.

These effects are represented through an effective roughness length:

```math
z_0
```

The EQM applies over the complete water surface, including both the windward and leeward sides.

The WPM pressure force, in contrast, is applied only to the windward side.

---

## Mechanistic nature of MOSD

MOSD is described as a **mechanistic model**.

A mechanistic model is based on a simplified physical explanation of the process rather than only on a fitted relationship obtained from experimental data.

The WPM coefficient:

```math
\frac{1}{\pi}
```

is obtained from the potential-flow derivation. It is not adjusted separately for each validation case.

Therefore, the WPM does not require a new empirical drag coefficient for every wavelength or wave condition.

However, the model still requires input information, including:

- the prescribed water-surface elevation;
- the air velocity;
- the wave velocity;
- the effective roughness of unresolved surface features.

Therefore, “no calibration” does not mean that the model needs no input data. It means that the main WPM pressure coefficient is physically derived instead of being fitted separately for every case.

## Main validation results

MOSD was compared with:

- wave-resolved Large-Eddy Simulations;
- Direct Numerical Simulations;
- laboratory experiments.

For most monochromatic-wave cases, MOSD reproduced the effect of the waves on the mean velocity profile.

The model also gave reasonable predictions of:

- Reynolds stresses;
- total surface drag;
- changes in drag with wave steepness;
- changes in drag with wave age;
- the reduction of drag for misaligned waves.

### Phase-dependent results

MOSD reproduced the principal phase-dependent airflow patterns.

The model predicted:

- acceleration on the windward side;
- acceleration near the wave crest;
- deceleration on the leeward side;
- upward motion before the crest;
- downward motion after the crest;
- alternating wave-induced Reynolds stresses.

These results show that MOSD retains information that would be lost in a simple phase-averaged roughness model.

However, the magnitude of the vertical motion was underpredicted.

```math
\text{wave-induced pattern}
\longrightarrow
\text{captured qualitatively}
```

```math
\text{strength of vertical motion}
\longrightarrow
\text{underpredicted}
```

### Multiple and misaligned waves

The model was also applied to:

- two aligned waves;
- one wave travelling at 45 degrees to the wind;
- one wave travelling at 90 degrees to the wind;
- two misaligned waves with different velocities.

The results followed the expected physical trends:

- a sufficiently steep second wave increased the drag;
- a very small second wave produced little additional drag;
- wave misalignment reduced the streamwise drag;
- a wave travelling perpendicular to the wind produced very little streamwise pressure drag.

These cases demonstrate that MOSD can be applied to complicated moving surfaces.

However, they were demonstrations rather than full validations because corresponding experimental or high-resolution numerical data were not available.

### Offshore wind-turbine wake

MOSD was applied to LES of an offshore wind turbine above moving waves.

The model reproduced the main wave-dependent changes in the turbine wake:

- the wake compressed and shortened as the wave crest moved underneath it;
- the wake length changed with wave phase;
- the wave-induced pumping motion increased momentum in the lower wake;
- the wake expanded again when the pumping effect weakened.

The simulation showed good qualitative agreement with wind-tunnel measurements.

This demonstrates that MOSD can be used to investigate wind–wave–wake interactions without resolving the complete air–water interface.

---

## Main strengths of MOSD

The principal strengths of the model are:

- it retains phase-dependent information about large waves;
- it is much less expensive than wave-resolved simulations;
- it does not require a wave-following grid;
- it can be implemented as a lower-boundary stress condition;
- it distinguishes resolved large waves from unresolved small waves;
- it can be applied to waves moving in different directions;
- it can be applied to surfaces containing multiple wavelengths;
- it gives good mean-flow predictions for most validation cases;
- its main WPM coefficient is physically derived rather than fitted.

The required WPM inputs are relatively simple:

```math
\boldsymbol{u}
```

for the local air velocity,

```math
\eta(x,y,t)
```

for the prescribed surface elevation,

```math
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
```

for the local wave slope, and:

```math
\boldsymbol{C}
```

for the local velocity of the wave surface.

## Main modelling assumptions

MOSD contains several strong simplifying assumptions.

### Potential-flow assumption

The WPM assumes ideal potential flow over each local wave section.

Potential flow neglects:

- viscosity;
- turbulent rotation;
- detailed flow separation;
- some near-surface turbulent effects.

The real airflow over ocean waves is turbulent. Therefore, the potential-flow solution is only an approximation used to estimate pressure.

### Local ramp assumption

Inside each LES cell, the wave surface is approximated as an inclined plane or ramp.

```math
\text{curved wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{collection of local ramps}
```

Each ramp is treated independently.

The model assumes that the airflow approaches every local ramp horizontally.

This is a strong simplification because the airflow may already have been deflected by the upstream part of the wave.

In reality:

```math
\text{upstream wave section}
\longrightarrow
\text{changes airflow direction}
\longrightarrow
\text{affects downstream section}
```

In the local MOSD approximation:

```math
\text{each cell}
\longrightarrow
\text{treated independently}
```

Therefore, MOSD does not retain the complete history of how the upstream surface has changed the airflow.

### Local versus non-local model

MOSD is a **local model** because the stress in one grid cell is calculated using only information available in that cell.

```math
\text{local air velocity}
+
\text{local slope}
+
\text{local wave velocity}
\longrightarrow
\text{local stress}
```

A more detailed non-local model would also consider how upstream wave sections influence the airflow reaching the current cell.

Such a model would require solving additional differential equations and would increase the computational cost.

The authors intentionally use a local model to keep MOSD simple and inexpensive.

### Leeward-pressure assumption

MOSD assumes that the pressure force on the leeward side is negligible because the airflow separates or nearly separates from the surface.

This assumption is reasonable for many ordinary wind-wave conditions.

However, it may become inaccurate for:

- very fast waves;
- small relative air–wave velocities;
- waves for which flow separation does not occur;
- waves near breaking conditions.

### One-way coupling

The water-surface elevation is prescribed:

```math
\eta(x,y,t)
```

The prescribed waves influence the airflow, but the simulated airflow does not modify the waves.

```math
\text{waves}
\longrightarrow
\text{airflow}
```

The reverse interaction is not calculated:

```math
\text{airflow}
\mathrel{\not\longrightarrow}
\text{wave evolution}
```

Therefore, the model does not calculate:

- wind-driven wave growth;
- changes in wave amplitude;
- nonlinear wave interactions;
- wave deformation;
- wave breaking.

---

## Main limitations observed in the results

The validation identified several limitations:

- wave-induced vertical velocities were underpredicted;
- the drag was underpredicted in one fully coupled DNS case;
- momentum transfer from extremely fast waves to the air was overpredicted;
- the model was not validated for a complete broadband ocean spectrum;
- the model was not tested for breaking waves;
- accuracy for steep waves remains uncertain;
- each local wave section is treated independently;
- the model uses prescribed waves and one-way coupling.

These limitations do not mean that the model is unusable. They identify the conditions under which its predictions should be interpreted carefully.

---

## Future work

The authors identify several important directions for future research.

### Realistic ocean spectra

Future studies should test MOSD with broadband wave fields containing many wavelengths, directions and velocities.

```math
\eta(x,y,t)
=
\sum_{m=1}^{M}
\eta_m(x,y,t)
```

where:

- $\eta$ is the complete water surface;
- $\eta_m$ is an individual wave component;
- $M$ is the number of wave components.

Although MOSD can mathematically accept such a surface, its accuracy for fully realistic ocean spectra still needs to be validated.

### Large offshore wind farms

The model should be applied to large offshore wind farms to study how moving waves influence:

- turbine wakes;
- wake recovery;
- interactions between turbines;
- atmospheric turbulence across the farm.

### Improved vertical motions

Future modifications should improve the prediction of wave-induced vertical velocity.

This is especially important for turbine wakes because vertical motion controls how high-momentum air is transported into the wake.

### Steep and breaking waves

The model should be tested for larger wave slopes and conditions approaching wave breaking.

Potential flow and the local-ramp approximation may become less accurate in these conditions.

### Non-local surface effects

A future model could include information from neighbouring or upstream cells.

This would allow the airflow direction arriving at each wave section to depend on previous deflections.

However, including this information would make the model more complicated and computationally expensive.

---

## Final conclusion

MOSD provides a useful compromise between physical detail and computational efficiency.

```math
\boxed{
\text{MOSD}
=
\text{resolved large-wave pressure drag}
+
\text{unresolved small-scale friction}
}
```

It is more informative than a simple effective-roughness model because it retains wave-phase information.

It is less expensive than DNS or wave-resolved LES because it does not resolve the complete near-surface airflow or require a wave-following grid.

The model performs well for many ordinary wave conditions and reproduces the main trends of wind–wave and wind–wave–turbine interaction.

Its predictions should be treated more carefully for:

- extremely fast waves;
- steep waves;
- breaking waves;
- fully broadband ocean spectra;
- situations in which accurate vertical wave-induced motion is essential.

The central value of MOSD is therefore:

```math
\text{important phase-dependent physics}
+
\text{simple wall boundary condition}
+
\text{low computational cost}
```
