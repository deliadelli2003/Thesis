## 6. Comparison with wind-tunnel data for an offshore wind turbine

The previous sections tested MOSD for airflow over waves without a wind turbine.

Section 6 investigates a more practical situation:

```math
\text{wind}
+
\text{moving waves}
+
\text{wind turbine}
```

The purpose is to determine whether MOSD can reproduce the influence of moving waves on the wake behind an offshore wind turbine.

The numerical results are compared with measurements from a wind-and-water tunnel experiment.

---

## 6.1 Experimental setup

### Wind-and-water tunnel

The experiment was performed in a closed-loop wind-and-water tunnel.

The test section had:

- a height of 0.8 metres;
- a width of 1.2 metres;
- a length of 5 metres;
- a water depth of 0.3 metres.

The free-stream wind velocity was:

```math
U_{\infty}
=
6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
```

where $U_{\infty}$ is the wind velocity before it reaches the turbine.

A wave paddle was installed at the entrance of the test section. The paddle moved the water periodically and produced deep-water waves travelling in one direction.

The experiment used one principal wavelength to isolate the interaction between:

- atmospheric turbulence;
- moving water waves;
- the turbine wake.

### Scaled wind turbine

The turbine-model rotor diameter was:

```math
D
=
0.15\ \mathrm{m}
```

where $D$ is the rotor diameter.

The geometrical scale was approximately:

```math
1:600
```

Therefore, the 0.15-metre model represented a full-scale turbine with a rotor diameter of approximately 90 metres.

The turbine was fixed to the bottom of the experimental water tank.

### Thrust coefficient

The turbine thrust coefficient was approximately:

```math
C_T
\approx
0.65
```

The thrust coefficient is defined as:

```math
C_T
=
\frac{T}
{
\frac{1}{2}
\rho
U_{\infty}^2
A
}
```

where:

- $C_T$ is the turbine thrust coefficient;
- $T$ is the thrust force exerted by the rotor on the airflow;
- $\rho$ is the air density;
- $U_{\infty}$ is the free-stream wind velocity;
- $A$ is the rotor-swept area.

The rotor area is:

```math
A
=
\pi R^2
```

where $R$ is the rotor radius.

The thrust coefficient measures how strongly the rotor slows the incoming wind.

### Tip-speed ratio

The turbine operated at a tip-speed ratio of:

```math
\mathrm{TSR}
=
5
```

The tip-speed ratio is:

```math
\mathrm{TSR}
=
\frac{\Omega R}{U_{\infty}}
```

where:

- $\Omega$ is the angular velocity of the rotor;
- $R$ is the rotor radius;
- $U_{\infty}$ is the free-stream wind velocity.

The tip-speed ratio compares the velocity of the blade tip with the incoming wind velocity.

### Wave parameters

The wave period was approximately:

```math
T
=
0.8\ \mathrm{s}
```

where $T$ is the time required for one complete wave cycle.

The measured phase velocity was approximately:

```math
c
=
1.2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
```

where $c$ is the horizontal propagation velocity of the wave.

The measured wavelength was approximately:

```math
\lambda
=
1\ \mathrm{m}
```

where $\lambda$ is the distance between two successive wave crests.

The relationship between wavelength, period and phase velocity is approximately:

```math
c
=
\frac{\lambda}{T}
```

Using the experimental values gives:

```math
c
\approx
\frac{1}{0.8}
=
1.25\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
```

which is close to the measured value.

### Experimental measurements

The water-surface elevation was measured using **Light-Induced Fluorescence**, abbreviated as **LIF**.

LIF uses fluorescent light to identify the position and shape of the air–water interface.

The airflow velocity was measured using **Particle Image Velocimetry**, abbreviated as **PIV**.

PIV records the movement of small particles carried by the airflow and uses their displacement to calculate the velocity field.

The measurements were performed in vertical planes aligned with the wind:

```math
x-z
```

where:

- $x$ is the streamwise direction;
- $z$ is the vertical direction.

The measured friction velocity was:

```math
u_*
=
0.26\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
```

where $u_*$ is the characteristic velocity associated with the total surface stress.

The boundary-layer height was approximately:

```math
h_{\mathrm{bl}}
=
0.2\ \mathrm{m}
```

where $h_{\mathrm{bl}}$ is the atmospheric boundary-layer height in the experiment.

## 6.2 MOSD-based WMLES setup

### Numerical representation of the turbine

The simulation does not directly resolve the rotating turbine blades.

Instead, the rotor is represented using an **Actuator Disk Model**, abbreviated as **ADM**.

The actuator disk replaces the rotating blades with a circular region that removes momentum from the airflow.

```math
\text{real rotating blades}
\longrightarrow
\text{equivalent momentum force}
```

The actuator disk produces a velocity deficit behind the turbine, representing the turbine wake.

The local thrust coefficient used by the actuator disk is:

```math
C_T'
=
\frac{C_T}{(1-a_{\mathrm{ind}})^2}
```

where:

- $C_T'$ is the local thrust coefficient used by the actuator-disk model;
- $C_T$ is the turbine thrust coefficient;
- $a_{\mathrm{ind}}$ is the axial induction factor.

The induction factor was:

```math
a_{\mathrm{ind}}
=
\frac{1}{4}
```

The resulting local thrust coefficient was approximately:

```math
C_T'
=
1.15
```

The induction factor represents the reduction in wind velocity caused by the rotor.

### Nacelle representation

The nacelle is the central body located behind the rotor.

Its drag was represented using a separate drag model with:

```math
C_D
=
12
```

where $C_D$ is the nacelle drag coefficient used in the simulation.

This value was selected to reproduce a velocity deficit near the centre of the turbine similar to that observed experimentally.

Therefore, the numerical turbine contains:

```math
\text{actuator disk}
+
\text{nacelle drag model}
```

The actuator disk represents the rotor, while the drag model represents the nacelle.

### Two computational domains

The simulation uses two computational domains:

1. a **precursor domain**;
2. a **wind-turbine domain**.

The precursor domain generates a fully developed turbulent atmospheric flow.

The turbine domain contains:

- the moving waves;
- the turbine actuator disk;
- the nacelle model;
- the turbine wake.

The procedure can be summarized as:

```math
\text{precursor domain}
\longrightarrow
\text{turbulent inflow}
\longrightarrow
\text{wind-turbine domain}
```

At every time step, the turbulent velocity generated in the precursor domain is supplied to the turbine domain.

This ensures that the wind reaching the turbine already contains realistic turbulent fluctuations.

### Fringe region

A fringe region is used near the end of the wind-turbine domain.

Inside this region, the simulated turbine flow is smoothly changed back into the prescribed turbulent inflow.

The fringe region allows periodic numerical boundary conditions to be used without sending the turbine wake directly back to the inlet.

```math
\text{turbine wake}
\longrightarrow
\text{fringe transition}
\longrightarrow
\text{turbulent inflow}
```

### Domain and flow parameters

The vertical domain size is:

```math
L_z
=
h_{\mathrm{bl}}
=
0.2\ \mathrm{m}
```

where:

- $L_z$ is the vertical size of the computational domain;
- $h_{\mathrm{bl}}$ is the boundary-layer height.

The air kinematic viscosity is:

```math
\nu
=
1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}
```

where $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity of air.

The imposed pressure gradient is selected so that the simulation reproduces the experimental friction velocity:

```math
u_*
=
0.26\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
```

MOSD is applied at the lower boundary in both the precursor domain and the turbine domain.

### Large waves and small ripples

The experiment contained:

- one main wave generated by the wave paddle;
- small wind-generated ripples on top of the principal wave.

The main wave was large enough to be represented by the LES and was therefore treated with the Windward Potential Flow Model.

```math
\text{main resolved wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{WPM pressure drag}
```

The smaller ripples were too small to be represented by the computational grid. Their combined effect was therefore included through the Equilibrium Wall Model.

```math
\text{unresolved ripples}
\longrightarrow
\text{EQM roughness stress}
```

The measured root-mean-square ripple height was:

```math
\eta_{\mathrm{rms}}'
=
2.58\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m}
```

where $\eta_{\mathrm{rms}}'$ is the characteristic height of the small unresolved ripples.

This height was approximately 6 percent of the amplitude of the main paddle-generated wave.

The ripple height is converted into an effective roughness length:

```math
z_0
=
\eta_{\mathrm{rms}}'
e^{-8.5\kappa}
```

where:

- $z_0$ is the effective roughness length;
- $\eta_{\mathrm{rms}}'$ is the root-mean-square ripple height;
- $\kappa$ is the von Kármán constant;
- $\kappa\approx0.4$.

The complete surface treatment is therefore:

```math
\text{large paddle wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{phase-dependent pressure drag}
```

```math
\text{small wind-generated ripples}
\longrightarrow
\text{average roughness effect}
```

This is an important practical example of the two MOSD components working together.

## 6.3 Results

### Validation of the incoming airflow

Before adding the turbine, the authors first simulated only the wind and waves.

The purpose was to verify that the airflow entering the turbine domain matched the experimental airflow.

They compared:

- the mean streamwise velocity;
- the streamwise velocity variance.

The mean streamwise velocity is:

```math
\langle u\rangle
```

The streamwise velocity variance is:

```math
\langle u'u'\rangle
```

where:

- $u$ is the instantaneous streamwise velocity;
- $\langle u\rangle$ is the mean streamwise velocity;
- $u'=u-\langle u\rangle$ is the turbulent streamwise fluctuation.

The MOSD simulation agrees well with the experimental turbulence statistics, especially in the logarithmic region of the velocity profile.

This confirms that the simulated turbulent inflow is suitable for studying the turbine wake.

### Turbine wake

The turbine removes momentum from the airflow and produces a region of reduced velocity behind the rotor.

This region is called the **wake**.

```math
\text{incoming wind}
\longrightarrow
\text{turbine}
\longrightarrow
\text{reduced-velocity wake}
```

The wake gradually recovers as surrounding higher-momentum air mixes into it.

Moving waves can modify this recovery by producing additional organized air motion below the wake.

### Phase-averaged wake

The wake was examined at four wave phases:

```math
\phi_1
=
\frac{\pi}{2}
```

```math
\phi_2
=
\pi
```

```math
\phi_3
=
\frac{3\pi}{2}
```

```math
\phi_4
=
2\pi
```

where $\phi$ identifies the position of the wave during one complete cycle.

At:

```math
\phi=0=2\pi
```

the wave crest is located directly below the turbine rotor.

Phase averaging makes it possible to isolate changes in the turbine wake caused by the passage of the wave.

### Comparison between LES and experiment

The LES reproduces the main phase-dependent changes observed experimentally.

However, the simulated wake is:

- slightly weaker;
- slightly thinner;
- more horizontal.

The experimental wake has a small downward inclination.

Possible causes of these differences include:

- the simplified actuator-disk representation of the rotor;
- the simplified nacelle model;
- differences between the experimental and numerical outer-flow conditions;
- the underprediction of wave-induced vertical velocity by MOSD.

Despite these differences, the variation of the wake with wave phase agrees qualitatively with the experiment.

### Effect of the wave crest

As the wave crest moves underneath the wake, it produces a region of increased airflow velocity near the lower part of the wake.

The increased-velocity region follows the moving wave crest.

This creates a concave deformation in the lower boundary of the wake.

The effect is related to the wave-induced pumping motion:

```math
\text{moving wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{wave-induced airflow}
\longrightarrow
\text{additional momentum below the wake}
```

### Wake compression and recovery

Both the experiment and the simulation show that the wake changes length as the wave passes underneath it.

The wave can increase the momentum of the air inside the lower part of the wake.

This causes the wake to recover more quickly and become shorter.

```math
\text{wave-induced momentum}
\longrightarrow
\text{faster wake recovery}
\longrightarrow
\text{shorter wake}
```

When the wave-induced momentum becomes weaker, the wake becomes longer again.

This periodic shortening and lengthening is caused by the wave-pumping effect.

### Definition of wake length

The wake length is identified using a velocity threshold:

```math
\frac{u}{U_{\infty}}
=
0.4
```

where:

- $u$ is the local streamwise velocity inside the wake;
- $U_{\infty}$ is the free-stream wind velocity.

The wake is considered to extend through the region where the velocity remains below 40 percent of the free-stream velocity.

The downstream position is expressed as:

```math
\frac{x}{D}
```

where:

- $x$ is the downstream distance from the turbine;
- $D$ is the turbine rotor diameter.

For example:

```math
\frac{x}{D}
=
3.3
```

means that the position is 3.3 rotor diameters downstream of the turbine.

### Variation with wave phase

The longest wake occurs approximately at:

```math
\phi_1
=
\frac{\pi}{2}
```

The shortest wake occurs approximately at:

```math
\phi_2
=
\pi
```

This phase-dependent change is found in both the LES and the experiment.

As the crest moves downstream to approximately:

```math
\frac{x}{D}
\approx
3.3
```

the wave-pumping effect weakens and the wake begins to increase in length again.

Therefore, the wave does not produce a constant effect on the wake. Its influence changes according to the position of the wave crest.

### Why the agreement is mainly qualitative

MOSD applies a horizontal surface stress but does not directly impose vertical motion.

The vertical airflow is generated indirectly through mass conservation:

```math
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}
=
0
```

The model captures the correct upward and downward motion pattern, but it underpredicts its magnitude.

Because vertical motion is important for bringing high-momentum air into the turbine wake, this underprediction contributes to differences in wake strength and shape.

---

## Main results of Section 6

The comparison shows that MOSD-based WMLES can reproduce the main effects of moving waves on an offshore wind-turbine wake.

The model reproduces:

- the mean velocity profile above the waves;
- the turbulence intensity of the incoming airflow;
- the phase-dependent deformation of the turbine wake;
- the shortening and lengthening of the wake;
- the influence of the moving wave crest;
- the wave-induced pumping effect.

The agreement with the experiment is mainly qualitative for the detailed wake structure.

The principal differences are:

- the simulated wake is weaker and thinner;
- the simulated wake has less downward inclination;
- the vertical wave-induced motion is underpredicted.

### Core concept

The wave affects the airflow below the turbine wake. This wave-induced airflow changes how quickly the wake recovers.

```math
\boxed{
\text{moving wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{wave-induced pumping}
\longrightarrow
\text{momentum added to the wake}
\longrightarrow
\text{phase-dependent wake recovery}
}
```

Section 6 demonstrates that MOSD can be used not only to study wind over waves, but also to study practical wind–wave–turbine interactions at a relatively low computational cost.

Source: [Ayala et al., *A Moving Surface Drag Model for LES of Wind Over Waves*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10546-024-00884-8)
