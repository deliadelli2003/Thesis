## 3. Implementation of MOSD in LES of wind over waves

The MOSD model is implemented in a Large-Eddy Simulation code called **LESGO**.

LESGO simulates turbulent flows in the atmospheric boundary layer. In this study, MOSD is applied at the lower boundary of the computational domain to represent the effect of the moving water surface on the airflow.

The simulation therefore combines:

```math
\text{LES of the atmospheric flow}
+
\text{MOSD boundary condition}
```

The LES calculates the large turbulent motions in the air, while MOSD calculates the stress produced by the waves at the lower boundary.

---

## 3.1 Governing equations and numerical method

### Filtered Navier–Stokes equations

LESGO solves the filtered Navier–Stokes equations.

Filtering separates the turbulent motions into:

- large motions that are directly resolved by the computational grid;
- small motions that are represented with a subgrid-scale model.

```math
\text{turbulent motion}
=
\text{resolved motion}
+
\text{subgrid motion}
```

### Continuity equation

For incompressible airflow, conservation of mass is expressed as:

```math
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}
=
0
```

where:

- $u_i$ is the filtered air-velocity component in direction $i$;
- $x_i$ is the spatial coordinate in direction $i$;
- the repeated index $i$ indicates summation over the three spatial directions.

The three velocity components are:

```math
u_i=(u,v,w)
```

where:

- $u$ is the streamwise velocity component;
- $v$ is the lateral velocity component;
- $w$ is the vertical velocity component.

The continuity equation means that the filtered airflow is divergence-free. For incompressible air, fluid cannot accumulate or disappear inside a computational cell.

### Momentum equation

The filtered momentum equation is written in rotational form:

```math
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t}
+
u_j
\left(
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}
-
\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
\right)
=
-
\frac{1}{\rho}
\frac{\partial P_{\infty}}{\partial x_1}
-
\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_i}
-
\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j}
+
\nu
\frac{\partial^2u_i}{\partial x_j^2}
```

where:

- $u_i$ is the filtered velocity component in direction $i$;
- $u_j$ represents the velocity components involved in the transport of momentum;
- $t$ is time;
- $x_i$ and $x_j$ are spatial coordinates;
- $\rho$ is the air density;
- $P_{\infty}$ is the imposed pressure responsible for driving the mean airflow;
- $P$ is the modified pressure inside the simulation;
- $\tau_{ij}$ is the subgrid-scale stress tensor;
- $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity of air;
- $i$ identifies the direction of the momentum equation;
- $j$ is a repeated index representing summation over the spatial directions.

The different terms represent:

```math
\text{change of momentum}
+
\text{transport by the airflow}
=
\text{driving pressure}
+
\text{local pressure}
+
\text{subgrid stress}
+
\text{viscous diffusion}
```

The rotational form is used because it helps the numerical method conserve mass and kinetic energy.

### Modified pressure

The modified pressure is:

```math
P
=
\frac{P_*}{\rho}
+
\frac{1}{3}\sigma_{kk}
+
\frac{1}{2}u_i u_i
```

where:

- $P$ is the modified pressure used in the momentum equation;
- $P_*$ is the physical air pressure;
- $\rho$ is the air density;
- $\sigma_{kk}$ is the trace of the complete subgrid-scale stress tensor;
- $u_i u_i/2$ is the resolved kinetic energy per unit mass.

The pressure is modified because part of the subgrid stress and part of the nonlinear velocity term can be included in a single pressure variable.

This simplifies the momentum equation without changing its physical meaning.

### Subgrid-scale stress

The LES grid resolves the large turbulent motions but cannot directly resolve turbulent motions smaller than the grid cells.

The influence of these unresolved motions is represented by the subgrid-scale stress:

```math
\tau_{ij}
=
-2\nu_t S_{ij}
```

where:

- $\tau_{ij}$ is the deviatoric subgrid-scale stress;
- $\nu_t$ is the turbulent eddy viscosity;
- $S_{ij}$ is the resolved strain-rate tensor.

The strain-rate tensor is:

```math
S_{ij}
=
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}
+
\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
\right)
```

where $S_{ij}$ measures how the resolved airflow deforms locally.

The subgrid model assumes that the small unresolved eddies act similarly to an additional viscosity.

```math
\text{molecular viscosity}
\longrightarrow
\text{diffusion caused by molecular motion}
```

```math
\text{eddy viscosity}
\longrightarrow
\text{diffusion caused by unresolved turbulence}
```

### Turbulent eddy viscosity

The turbulent eddy viscosity is calculated using a Smagorinsky-type model:

```math
\nu_t
=
(C_S\Delta)^2
\sqrt{S_{ij}S_{ij}}
```

where:

- $\nu_t$ is the turbulent eddy viscosity;
- $C_S$ is the Smagorinsky model coefficient;
- $\Delta$ is the characteristic grid-filter size;
- $S_{ij}S_{ij}$ represents the magnitude of the resolved strain rate.

The characteristic grid size is:

```math
\Delta
=
(\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z)^{1/3}
```

where:

- $\Delta x$ is the grid spacing in the streamwise direction;
- $\Delta y$ is the grid spacing in the lateral direction;
- $\Delta z$ is the grid spacing in the vertical direction.

A larger strain rate produces a larger turbulent viscosity. This means that stronger resolved deformation creates stronger transport by the unresolved turbulent motions.

The coefficient $C_S$ is not kept constant. It is calculated dynamically from the local flow using a Lagrangian scale-dependent model.

This allows the subgrid model to adapt to changes in the turbulence.

### Numerical methods

Different numerical methods are used in different directions.

In the streamwise and lateral directions, the code uses a pseudo-spectral method.

In the vertical direction, it uses a second-order centred finite-difference method.

Time is advanced using a second-order Adams–Bashforth method.

The main numerical choices are therefore:

- pseudo-spectral discretization in the horizontal directions;
- finite differences in the vertical direction;
- second-order time integration;
- shifted periodic boundary conditions in the horizontal directions;
- a stress-free condition at the top boundary;
- the MOSD wall condition at the bottom boundary.

The shifted periodic boundary condition prevents the formation of artificially long turbulent structures caused by ordinary periodic boundaries.

### Application of MOSD at the lower boundary

MOSD is applied at the bottom of the computational domain.

The velocity supplied to the wall model is test-filtered over a length scale:

```math
2\Delta
```

where $\Delta$ is the characteristic LES grid size.

This additional filtering removes small variations that should not directly affect an equilibrium wall model, which is intended to represent the mean near-surface behaviour.

For the Equilibrium Wall Model, the input velocity is taken at:

```math
z
=
2.5\Delta z
```

where:

- $z$ is the vertical position above the mean surface;
- $\Delta z$ is the vertical grid spacing.

This position corresponds approximately to the third vertical grid point.

The velocity is not taken directly at the first grid point because the equilibrium wall model works better when its input is taken farther from the surface. This helps reduce the **log-layer mismatch**, which is an error between the velocity profile predicted by the wall model and the velocity profile calculated by the LES.

### Driving the atmospheric flow

The atmospheric boundary-layer flow is driven by a constant pressure gradient:

```math
-
\frac{1}{\rho}
\frac{\partial P_{\infty}}{\partial x_1}
=
\frac{u_*^2}{h_{\mathrm{bl}}}
```

where:

- $\rho$ is the air density;
- $P_{\infty}$ is the imposed driving pressure;
- $x_1$ is the streamwise coordinate;
- $u_*$ is the friction velocity;
- $h_{\mathrm{bl}}$ is the boundary-layer height.

The negative pressure gradient forces the air to move in the streamwise direction.

The friction velocity $u_*$ is a velocity scale connected to the total surface stress:

```math
u_*
=
\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\rho}}
```

where:

- $\tau$ is the characteristic surface stress;
- $\rho$ is the air density.

The simulations continue until the flow reaches a statistically steady state.

A statistically steady state does not mean that the instantaneous turbulent velocity stops changing. It means that average quantities, such as mean velocity and kinetic energy, no longer change significantly with time.

## 3.2 Simulation setup

### Prescribed monochromatic wave

The first validation simulations use a monochromatic wave.

A monochromatic wave contains one principal wavelength, frequency and phase speed. It can be written as:

```math
\eta(x,t)
=
a\cos(kx-\omega t)
```

where:

- $\eta$ is the water-surface elevation;
- $a$ is the wave amplitude;
- $k$ is the wavenumber;
- $\omega$ is the angular frequency;
- $x$ is the streamwise position;
- $t$ is time.

The wavelength is:

```math
\lambda
=
\frac{2\pi}{k}
```

where:

- $\lambda$ is the wavelength;
- $k$ is the wavenumber.

The phase velocity is:

```math
c
=
\frac{\omega}{k}
```

where:

- $c$ is the wave phase velocity;
- $\omega$ is the angular frequency;
- $k$ is the wavenumber.

The wave moves in the positive streamwise direction.

### Position of the wave below the wall-model point

The velocity used by the wall model is taken at the third vertical grid point:

```math
\Delta z_3
```

where $\Delta z_3$ is the height of the third grid point above the mean water level.

The complete wave must remain below this point. Therefore, the wave amplitude must satisfy:

```math
a
\leq
0.99\Delta z_3
```

where:

- $a$ is the maximum wave elevation above the mean surface;
- $\Delta z_3$ is the height of the wall-model sampling point.

This condition ensures that the wall-model velocity is always taken above the wave surface.

If the wave reached or crossed the sampling point, the model would attempt to use a velocity located inside or too close to the water surface, making the wall-model assumption invalid.

### Computational-domain dimensions

The vertical domain size is equal to the boundary-layer height:

```math
L_z
=
h_{\mathrm{bl}}
```

where:

- $L_z$ is the vertical size of the computational domain;
- $h_{\mathrm{bl}}$ is the atmospheric boundary-layer height.

All lengths are normalized using $h_{\mathrm{bl}}$. Therefore:

```math
L_z=1
```

in normalized units.

The horizontal dimensions are represented by:

- $L_x$, the streamwise domain length;
- $L_y$, the lateral domain length.

The domain is selected so that it contains at least four complete waves:

```math
L_x
\geq
4\lambda
```

where $\lambda$ is the wavelength.

Including several waves allows meaningful spatial averaging and reduces the influence of the periodic boundaries on an individual wave.

### Horizontal wave resolution

Each wavelength is represented by at least eight horizontal grid points:

```math
\frac{\lambda}{\Delta x}
\geq
8
```

where:

- $\lambda$ is the wavelength;
- $\Delta x$ is the streamwise grid spacing.

This does not mean that the complete airflow around the wave is resolved. It means that the wave position and slope are represented with enough horizontal points for MOSD to calculate their effect.

The grid-spacing ratios also satisfy:

```math
\frac{\Delta z}{\Delta x}
=
\frac{\Delta z}{\Delta y}
\geq
\frac{1}{6}
```

where:

- $\Delta x$ is the streamwise grid spacing;
- $\Delta y$ is the lateral grid spacing;
- $\Delta z$ is the vertical grid spacing.

This condition prevents the computational cells from becoming excessively stretched in the horizontal directions.

### Validation data

The MOSD simulations are compared with data from seven previous studies:

- five numerical studies;
- two experimental studies.

The numerical reference studies include:

- wave-resolved Large-Eddy Simulations;
- Direct Numerical Simulations;
- simulations using grids that follow the moving wave surface;
- fully coupled air–water simulations.

The experimental reference studies use Particle Image Velocimetry, abbreviated as **PIV**, to measure the airflow close to the wave surface.

PIV measures velocity by recording the motion of small particles carried by the airflow.

The purpose of these comparisons is to determine whether the less expensive MOSD wall model can reproduce the main results obtained using more detailed simulations and experiments.

### Wave conditions used for validation

The validation cases include different values of:

- wave steepness;
- wave phase velocity;
- Reynolds number;
- domain size;
- grid resolution.

The wave steepness is:

```math
ak
```

where:

- $a$ is the wave amplitude;
- $k$ is the wavenumber.

The friction Reynolds number is:

```math
Re_{\tau}
=
\frac{u_*h_{\mathrm{bl}}}{\nu}
```

where:

- $Re_{\tau}$ is the friction Reynolds number;
- $u_*$ is the friction velocity;
- $h_{\mathrm{bl}}$ is the boundary-layer height;
- $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity of air.

Using different wave steepnesses, wave velocities and Reynolds numbers tests whether MOSD works under a range of flow conditions.

### Smooth surface assumption

For the initial validation cases, the wave surface is treated as smooth:

```math
z_0=0
```

where $z_0$ is the effective roughness length.

This assumption is used because the reference cases contain no significant unresolved ripples or because the influence of those ripples on the airflow is negligible.

Therefore, these validation cases mainly test the Windward Potential Flow Model and the viscous part of the Equilibrium Wall Model.

### Core concept of Section 3

Section 3 explains how the theoretical MOSD stress is introduced into a practical LES.

```math
\text{filtered Navier–Stokes equations}
+
\text{subgrid turbulence model}
+
\text{MOSD lower boundary}
```

The simulation resolves the large atmospheric turbulent motions, models the smaller turbulent motions and uses MOSD to represent the influence of the moving water surface.

The model is then tested using waves and flow conditions taken from previous high-resolution simulations and laboratory experiments.

Source: [Ayala et al., *A Moving Surface Drag Model for LES of Wind Over Waves*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10546-024-00884-8)
