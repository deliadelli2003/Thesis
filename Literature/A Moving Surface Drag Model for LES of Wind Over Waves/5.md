## 5. Applicability beyond monochromatic aligned waves

The previous validation cases mainly considered one monochromatic wave moving in the same direction as the mean wind.

A monochromatic aligned wave has:

- one wavelength;
- one phase velocity;
- one propagation direction;
- a propagation direction aligned with the wind.

Real ocean surfaces are more complicated. They normally contain many waves with different:

- wavelengths;
- amplitudes;
- steepnesses;
- phase velocities;
- propagation directions.

A realistic water surface can therefore be considered a superposition of several wave components:

```math
\eta(x,y,t)
=
\eta_1(x,y,t)
+
\eta_2(x,y,t)
+
\eta_3(x,y,t)
+
\cdots
```

where:

- $\eta$ is the total water-surface elevation;
- $\eta_1$, $\eta_2$ and $\eta_3$ are individual wave components;
- $x$ and $y$ are the horizontal coordinates;
- $t$ is time.

The purpose of Section 5 is to show that MOSD can also be applied to these more complicated wave fields.

---

### Why MOSD can be applied to complex waves

MOSD does not need to calculate the drag of every individual wavelength separately.

Instead, it uses the total local surface elevation:

```math
\eta(x,y,t)
```

From the total elevation, MOSD calculates the local surface gradient:

```math
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
=
\left(
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x},
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}
\right)
```

where:

- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta$ is the gradient of the complete water surface;
- $\partial\eta/\partial x$ is the surface slope in the streamwise direction;
- $\partial\eta/\partial y$ is the surface slope in the lateral direction.

MOSD also calculates the local horizontal movement of the complete surface:

```math
C_i
=
-
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2}
```

where:

- $C_i$ is the local surface-velocity component in horizontal direction $i$;
- $\partial\eta/\partial t$ is the local change in surface elevation with time;
- $\partial\eta/\partial x_i$ is the local slope in direction $i$;
- $|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|$ is the magnitude of the total surface gradient.

Therefore, MOSD only needs the total shape and movement of the surface at each position.

```math
\text{complex wave field}
\longrightarrow
\text{local total slope and velocity}
\longrightarrow
\text{local MOSD stress}
```

This allows the same MOSD equation to be used for single waves, multiple waves and waves moving at an angle to the wind.

---

## Wave direction and pressure drag

The pressure drag depends on the relative air velocity projected onto the local wave-slope direction:

```math
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
```

where:

- $\boldsymbol{u}$ is the horizontal air velocity;
- $\boldsymbol{C}$ is the local horizontal wave velocity;
- $\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C}$ is the air velocity relative to the wave;
- $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ is the horizontal direction in which the surface rises most rapidly.

The slope direction is:

```math
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
=
\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta}
{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|}
```

The dot product is large when the relative airflow moves directly against the wave slope.

The dot product becomes small when the airflow moves almost parallel to the wave crest.

Therefore:

```math
\text{wind perpendicular to wave crest}
\longrightarrow
\text{large pressure drag}
```

```math
\text{wind parallel to wave crest}
\longrightarrow
\text{small pressure drag}
```

This makes the model naturally sensitive to the angle between the wind and waves.

---

## Wave scenarios investigated

The paper considers three general situations:

| Scenario | Wave configuration | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Double aligned waves | Two waves moving in the wind direction | Test the effect of multiple wavelengths and steepnesses |
| Single misaligned wave | One wave moving at an angle to the wind | Test the effect of wave direction |
| Double misaligned waves | Two waves moving obliquely at different velocities | Test multiple wavelengths and directions together |

The angle between the wave-propagation direction and the mean wind is represented by:

```math
\gamma
```

where:

- $\gamma=0^\circ$ means that the wave and wind move in the same direction;
- $\gamma=45^\circ$ means that the wave travels diagonally relative to the wind;
- $\gamma=90^\circ$ means that the wave travels perpendicular to the wind.

These simulations are illustrative. The authors did not have corresponding experimental or numerical data for direct validation.

Therefore, Section 5 demonstrates that the model can be applied to complex wave conditions, but it does not prove its accuracy for all realistic ocean-wave fields.

---

## 5.1 Two aligned waves

In the first scenario, two different waves move in the same direction as the mean wind.

The total surface is:

```math
\eta
=
\eta_{\mathrm{w1}}
+
\eta_{\mathrm{w2}}
```

where:

- $\eta_{\mathrm{w1}}$ is the elevation of the first wave;
- $\eta_{\mathrm{w2}}$ is the elevation of the second wave;
- $\eta$ is the combined water-surface elevation.

The two waves have different wavelengths and phase velocities.

Two cases are considered:

- **DAW07**, where the second wave has a small steepness;
- **DAW15**, where the second wave has a larger steepness.

For the smaller second wave:

```math
(ak)_{\mathrm{w2}}
=
0.07
```

For the steeper second wave:

```math
(ak)_{\mathrm{w2}}
=
0.15
```

where:

- $(ak)_{\mathrm{w2}}$ is the steepness of the second wave;
- $a$ is its amplitude;
- $k$ is its wavenumber.

### Results for two aligned waves

It may initially appear that adding a second wave should always increase the total drag.

However, the simulations show that the second wave produces a clear increase in drag only when it is sufficiently steep.

For the DAW07 case, the second wave has little influence on the mean velocity profile.

For the DAW15 case, the second wave produces a clear increase in surface drag and a larger reduction in the mean wind velocity.

This happens because the WPM stress approximately scales with the square of the wave steepness:

```math
\tau^{\mathrm{WPM}}
\sim
(ak)^2
```

where:

- $\tau^{\mathrm{WPM}}$ is the wave-pressure stress;
- $ak$ is the wave steepness.

Therefore, a small reduction in steepness can cause a much larger reduction in pressure drag.

```math
\text{small secondary wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{negligible additional drag}
```

```math
\text{steep secondary wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{significant additional drag}
```

The result shows that the number of waves alone does not determine the total drag. The steepness and relative velocity of each wave component are also important.

---

## 5.2 One misaligned wave

The second scenario considers one wave travelling at an angle relative to the mean wind.

Two angles are examined:

```math
\gamma=45^\circ
```

and:

```math
\gamma=90^\circ
```

where $\gamma$ is the angle between the wave-propagation direction and the mean wind.

### Wave travelling at 45 degrees

When the wave travels at an angle of 45 degrees, only part of the streamwise airflow moves directly against the wave slope.

The velocity component producing pressure drag is determined by:

```math
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
```

Because the wave slope is not directly opposed to the mean wind, this projected relative velocity is smaller than for an aligned wave.

Therefore, the wave produces less streamwise drag.

```math
\gamma=45^\circ
\longrightarrow
\text{reduced relative velocity against the slope}
\longrightarrow
\text{reduced drag}
```

The instantaneous velocity field still shows wave-related bands of acceleration and deceleration, but they are weaker than in the aligned case.

### Wave travelling at 90 degrees

When the wave travels perpendicular to the mean wind:

```math
\gamma=90^\circ
```

the mean streamwise airflow is approximately parallel to the wave crest.

Therefore, the streamwise air velocity does not strongly encounter the wave slope.

Only the lateral velocity component contributes significantly to the pressure drag:

```math
v
```

where $v$ is the lateral air-velocity component.

Because the mean lateral velocity is much smaller than the mean streamwise velocity:

```math
|v|
\ll
|u|
```

the resulting wave-pressure drag is small.

The simulations therefore show no clear streamwise bands of deceleration for the 90-degree case.

```math
\gamma=90^\circ
\longrightarrow
\text{wind approximately parallel to wave crest}
\longrightarrow
\text{small streamwise pressure drag}
```

### Mean-flow result for misaligned waves

Both misaligned cases produce less streamwise drag than the aligned-wave case.

This produces a larger mean streamwise velocity in the logarithmic region.

The result shows that wave direction is important even when wave amplitude, wavelength and phase velocity remain unchanged.

```math
\text{same wave parameters}
+
\text{different direction}
\longrightarrow
\text{different surface drag}
```

---

## 5.3 Two misaligned waves

The third scenario contains two waves:

- different wavelengths;
- different phase velocities;
- propagation at 45 degrees relative to the mean wind.

This case is called the **double misaligned wave case**, abbreviated as **DMW**.

The total surface is:

```math
\eta
=
\eta_{\mathrm{w1}}
+
\eta_{\mathrm{w2}}
```

Both wave components contribute to the local surface slope:

```math
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
=
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta_{\mathrm{w1}}
+
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta_{\mathrm{w2}}
```

where:

- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta$ is the gradient of the complete surface;
- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta_{\mathrm{w1}}$ is the gradient of the first wave;
- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta_{\mathrm{w2}}$ is the gradient of the second wave.

The interaction of the two wave shapes creates a more complicated local slope and pressure-stress distribution.

### Results for two misaligned waves

The mean velocity profile differs slightly from the corresponding case with two aligned waves.

The wave amplitudes, steepnesses and phase velocities are the same. Therefore, the difference is mainly caused by the direction of the waves relative to the wind.

Because the waves are misaligned, a smaller part of the mean airflow moves directly against their slopes.

This reduces their streamwise drag compared with the aligned case.

The result confirms that MOSD responds to:

- multiple wave components;
- different phase velocities;
- changing local slopes;
- wave direction relative to the wind.

---

## Interpretation of the instantaneous velocity fields

The effect of the waves appears in the near-surface airflow as regions or bands of reduced streamwise velocity.

For aligned waves, these bands are clearly related to the wave crests.

For waves travelling at 45 degrees, the bands are weaker and oblique.

For waves travelling at 90 degrees, the streamwise bands are almost absent because the mean wind does not directly encounter the wave slopes.

For two misaligned waves, the pattern is more complicated because the total surface contains two different wave components.

These results show that MOSD retains phase information even for complex surfaces.

A simple roughness model would only produce an average drag and could not show these local bands associated with the wave crests.

---

## Main results of Section 5

The simulations show that MOSD can be applied to:

- more than one wavelength;
- waves with different phase velocities;
- waves aligned with the wind;
- waves travelling diagonally relative to the wind;
- waves travelling perpendicular to the wind;
- combinations of multiple misaligned waves.

The principal physical results are:

1. A second wave only produces significant additional drag if its steepness is sufficiently large.

```math
\text{larger wave steepness}
\longrightarrow
\text{much larger pressure drag}
```

2. Misalignment between the wind and waves reduces the streamwise pressure drag.

```math
\text{larger wind-wave angle}
\longrightarrow
\text{smaller velocity component against the slope}
\longrightarrow
\text{smaller streamwise drag}
```

3. A wave travelling perpendicular to the wind produces very little streamwise form drag.

4. The local-slope formulation allows MOSD to handle complicated surfaces without calculating a separate empirical drag coefficient for every wavelength.

### Important limitation

The Section 5 cases are demonstrations of the model’s general applicability.

They are not direct validations because no corresponding reference simulations or experiments were available for comparison.

Therefore:

```math
\text{Section 5 demonstrates applicability}
\neq
\text{complete validation for realistic ocean spectra}
```

Further comparisons with experimental data or high-resolution simulations are required before the model can be considered fully validated for arbitrary ocean-wave fields.

### Core concept

MOSD calculates drag from the total local surface geometry and relative motion:

```math
\boxed{
\text{local surface slope}
+
\text{local surface velocity}
+
\text{local air velocity}
\longrightarrow
\text{local wave stress}
}
```

For this reason, the same model can be used for a simple sinusoidal wave or a complex surface containing several waves moving in different directions.

Source: [Ayala et al., *A Moving Surface Drag Model for LES of Wind Over Waves*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10546-024-00884-8)
