## 2. Moving Surface Drag model

The **Moving Surface Drag model**, abbreviated as **MOSD**, is a wall model for Large-Eddy Simulations of wind over moving water waves.

A Large-Eddy Simulation usually cannot reproduce all the small details of the airflow close to the water surface. MOSD therefore represents the effect of the waves as a stress applied to the air at the lower boundary of the simulation.

The total MOSD stress is divided into two contributions:

```math
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{MOSD}}
=
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
+
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{EQM}}
```

The first contribution is calculated with the **Windward Potential Flow Model**, abbreviated as **WPM**. It represents the pressure drag caused by the large waves included in the simulation.

The second contribution is calculated with the **Equilibrium Wall Model**, abbreviated as **EQM**. It represents the average effect of small unresolved waves, surface roughness and viscous friction.

In simple terms:

```math
\text{total surface stress}
=
\text{large-wave pressure drag}
+
\text{small-scale friction}
```

### Resolved and unresolved waves

The large waves are described by the water-surface elevation:

```math
\eta(x,y,t)
```

This function gives the height of the water surface at every horizontal position and time.

The large waves are **horizontally resolved**. This means that the model knows:

- their position;
- their slope;
- their direction;
- their movement.

However, the waves are **vertically unresolved**. This means that the computational mesh does not follow the complete curved shape of the water surface.

The simulation can therefore use a flat lower boundary. MOSD introduces the effect of the moving waves by applying the correct stress to the airflow.

MOSD is consequently a middle ground between two approaches:

- a simple roughness model, which only represents the average effect of the waves;
- a fully resolved simulation, which directly represents the detailed airflow around every wave.

MOSD retains information about the large moving waves without requiring a computational grid that follows the water surface.

## 2.1 Windward Potential Flow Model

The **Windward Potential Flow Model** calculates the pressure drag produced by the large resolved waves.

The basic idea is that the incoming airflow encounters the front slope of a wave and is forced to move upward. This change in the direction of the airflow creates increased pressure on the wave surface.

The wave then produces an opposite force on the airflow. In the simulation, this force appears as a surface stress.

### Local approximation of the wave

MOSD divides the water surface into small sections corresponding to the computational cells.

Inside each cell, the curved wave surface is approximated as a small inclined plane, similar to a ramp.

The local wave slope is calculated from the surface elevation:

```math
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
=
\left(
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x},
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}
\right)
```

The magnitude of the gradient gives the steepness of the local surface:

```math
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|
```

A steeper wave causes a stronger change in the direction of the airflow. It therefore produces a larger pressure force.

```math
\text{larger wave slope}
\longrightarrow
\text{stronger airflow deflection}
\longrightarrow
\text{larger pressure drag}
```

If the surface is locally flat, the wave-pressure drag is zero:

```math
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta=0
```

However, the total stress is not necessarily zero because friction and small unresolved waves are still represented by the Equilibrium Wall Model.

### Windward side

The **windward side** is the side of the wave that the relative airflow encounters first.

When the wind moves faster than the wave in the same direction, this is normally the front rising slope of the wave.

The air moves toward the surface and is forced upward. This creates increased pressure.

```math
\text{air moves toward the wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{airflow is deflected upward}
\longrightarrow
\text{pressure increases}
```

MOSD includes the pressure force on this side.

### Leeward side

The **leeward side** is the side behind the wave crest.

After the airflow passes over the crest, it moves away from the descending wave surface. The airflow may separate from the surface and create a sheltered region.

```math
\text{air passes the crest}
\longrightarrow
\text{air moves away from the surface}
\longrightarrow
\text{possible flow separation}
```

MOSD assumes that the pressure force on the leeward side is negligible. Therefore, this pressure contribution is not included in the model.

For wind moving from left to right and faster than the wave:

```math
\text{wind}
\longrightarrow
\text{windward slope}
\longrightarrow
\text{wave crest}
\longrightarrow
\text{leeward slope}
```

The pressure treatment is:

```math
\text{windward pressure}
=
\text{included}
```

```math
\text{leeward pressure}
\approx
0
```

The windward and leeward sides are determined by the airflow relative to the moving wave. Therefore, if the wave moves faster than the air, their roles can reverse.

### Relative air velocity

The wave surface is moving, so the pressure force cannot be calculated using only the air velocity.

The relevant quantity is the velocity of the air relative to the wave:

```math
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{rel}}
=
\boldsymbol{u}
-
\boldsymbol{C}
```

In this equation:

- the first vector is the air velocity obtained from the simulation;
- the second vector is the local velocity of the moving wave;
- the result is the velocity of the air as observed from the wave.

In simple terms:

```math
\text{relative velocity}
=
\text{air velocity}
-
\text{wave velocity}
```

If the wind is much faster than the wave, the relative velocity is large and the pressure drag is strong.

If the wind and wave move at almost the same velocity, the relative velocity is small and the pressure drag decreases.

If the wave moves faster than the wind, the relative airflow changes direction. The opposite wave slope may then become the windward side.

### Direction of the wave slope

The following unit vector gives the horizontal direction in which the water surface rises:

```math
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
=
\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta}
{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|}
```

Only the part of the relative airflow moving directly against the wave slope contributes to the pressure force.

This part is calculated using:

```math
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
```

A large value means that the airflow is moving strongly against the wave surface.

A value equal to zero means that the airflow is moving parallel to the wave crest. In this case, it does not directly encounter the slope.

### Local velocity of the wave

For a general moving water surface, the local horizontal wave velocity is calculated from the change in the surface elevation:

```math
C_i
=
-
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2}
```

This equation uses:

- the change in surface height over time;
- the local wave slope.

It determines how rapidly the local wave shape is moving horizontally.

For a simple sinusoidal wave:

```math
\eta(x,t)
=
a\cos(kx-\omega t)
```

the equation gives the usual wave phase velocity:

```math
C
=
\frac{\omega}{k}
```

This approach can also be applied to more complex surfaces containing waves with different wavelengths and directions.

### Potential-flow approximation

The airflow over each local inclined surface is approximated as **potential flow**.

Potential flow assumes that the flow is:

- inviscid, meaning that viscosity is neglected;
- irrotational, meaning that local fluid rotation is neglected.

The real airflow over waves is turbulent and viscous. Therefore, potential flow is not intended to describe the complete airflow.

It is used only to obtain a simple estimate of the pressure produced when the airflow is deflected by the large wave.

The effects of friction and unresolved small waves are included separately through the Equilibrium Wall Model.

### Pressure on the windward side

Bernoulli’s equation is used to estimate the average pressure increase on the windward side:

```math
\frac{\Delta\overline{P}}{\rho}
=
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|
```

The equation shows that the pressure increases when:

- the relative airflow against the wave becomes faster;
- the local wave surface becomes steeper.

The main physical relationship is:

```math
\text{wave pressure}
\sim
(\text{relative velocity})^2
\times
\text{wave slope}
```

The pressure is then converted into a horizontal stress acting on the airflow.

### Final wave-pressure stress

The stress produced by the large resolved waves is:

```math
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
=
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2
H
\left[
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
\right]
\hat{n}_i
```

The equation contains four main physical effects:

1. The relative velocity determines how rapidly the air encounters the moving wave.
2. The wave slope determines how strongly the air is deflected.
3. The slope direction determines the direction of the resulting force.
4. The switch function ensures that pressure drag is applied only on the windward side.

The switch function can be understood as:

```math
H
=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{windward side},\\
0, & \text{leeward side}.
\end{cases}
```

Therefore:

```math
\text{large-wave drag}
\sim
(\text{relative wind speed})^2
(\text{wave slope})^2
```

A steep wave encountering a fast relative airflow produces strong pressure drag. A flat surface or a surface moving at nearly the same velocity as the air produces little pressure drag.

### Main assumptions

The Windward Potential Flow Model makes the following simplifications:

- each small section of the wave is approximated as an inclined plane;
- every computational cell is treated locally and independently;
- potential flow is used to calculate the pressure;
- the local flow is treated as steady;
- the pressure force is included only on the windward side;
- the pressure force on the leeward side is neglected;
- the model is mainly intended for waves with relatively small slopes.

These assumptions make the model much less computationally expensive than a fully resolved simulation.

The Windward Potential Flow Model represents the **phase-dependent pressure drag of the large waves**. The Equilibrium Wall Model separately represents the **average frictional effect of the small unresolved waves**.
