## 2. Moving Surface Drag model

The **Moving Surface Drag model**, abbreviated as **MOSD**, is a wall model used in Large-Eddy Simulations of airflow over moving water waves.

A Large-Eddy Simulation cannot normally resolve all the small details of the airflow close to the water surface. MOSD therefore calculates the effect of the waves and applies it to the airflow as a surface stress.

The total MOSD stress contains two contributions:

```math
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{MOSD}}
=
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
+
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{EQM}}
```

where:

- $\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{MOSD}}$ is the total stress applied by the MOSD model;
- $\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}$ is the pressure stress produced by the large resolved waves;
- $\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{EQM}}$ is the equilibrium stress produced by surface friction and small unresolved waves;
- $i$ identifies one of the horizontal directions.

Therefore:

```math
\text{total surface stress}
=
\text{large-wave pressure drag}
+
\text{small-scale friction}
```

### Resolved and unresolved waves

The moving water surface is described by:

```math
\eta(x,y,t)
```

where:

- $\eta$ is the elevation of the water surface;
- $x$ and $y$ are the horizontal coordinates;
- $t$ is time.

The large waves are **horizontally resolved**. This means that the simulation knows their position, slope, direction and movement.

However, they are **vertically unresolved**. This means that the computational mesh does not follow the curved shape of the water surface.

The simulation can therefore use a flat lower boundary. The effect of the moving waves is introduced by applying a surface stress to the airflow.

The smaller waves cannot be represented individually because they are smaller than the computational grid. Their combined effect is included as an average surface roughness through the Equilibrium Wall Model.

MOSD is therefore a middle ground between:

- a simple roughness model, which only represents the average effect of the waves;
- a fully resolved simulation, which directly represents the detailed airflow around every wave.

MOSD retains information about the large moving waves without requiring a computational grid that follows the water surface.

## 2.1 Windward Potential Flow Model

The **Windward Potential Flow Model**, abbreviated as **WPM**, calculates the pressure drag produced by the large resolved waves.

The main idea is that the incoming airflow encounters the slope of a wave and is forced to change direction. This creates increased pressure on the wave surface.

The airflow pushes the wave, while the wave produces an opposite force on the airflow. In the simulation, this force is represented as a surface stress.

### Local approximation of the wave

MOSD divides the water surface into small sections corresponding to the computational cells.

Inside each cell, the curved wave surface is approximated as a small inclined plane, similar to a ramp.

The local wave slope is described by:

```math
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
=
\left(
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x},
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}
\right)
```

where:

- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta$ is the horizontal gradient of the water-surface elevation;
- $\partial\eta/\partial x$ is the surface slope in the $ $x$ direction;
- $\partial\eta/\partial y$ is the surface slope in the $y$ direction;
- $|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|$ is the magnitude of the gradient and represents the local wave steepness.

A steeper wave deflects the airflow more strongly and therefore produces a larger pressure force:

```math
\text{larger wave slope}
\longrightarrow
\text{stronger airflow deflection}
\longrightarrow
\text{larger pressure drag}
```

If the surface is locally flat:

```math
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta=0
```

the pressure drag produced by the large wave is zero.

The total surface stress may still be different from zero because surface friction and small unresolved waves are represented by the Equilibrium Wall Model.

### Windward and leeward sides

The **windward side** is the side of the wave that the relative airflow encounters first.

When the wind moves faster than the wave in the same direction, the windward side is normally the front rising slope. The airflow moves toward this surface and is forced upward, creating increased pressure.

```math
\text{air moves toward the wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{airflow is deflected upward}
\longrightarrow
\text{pressure increases}
```

MOSD includes the pressure force on the windward side.

The **leeward side** is the side behind the wave crest. After passing over the crest, the airflow moves away from the descending wave surface.

The airflow may separate from the surface and create a sheltered region behind the crest. MOSD therefore assumes that the pressure force on the leeward side is negligible.

```math
\text{air passes the crest}
\longrightarrow
\text{air moves away from the surface}
\longrightarrow
\text{possible flow separation}
```

For wind moving from left to right and faster than the wave:

```math
\text{wind}
\longrightarrow
\text{windward slope}
\longrightarrow
\text{wave crest}
\longrightarrow
\text{leeward slope}
```

The two sides are treated as follows:

```math
\text{windward pressure}
=
\text{included}
```

```math
\text{leeward pressure}
\approx
0
```

This distinction depends on the **relative airflow**, not only on the absolute direction of the wind. If the wave moves faster than the air, the relative airflow changes direction and the opposite slope may become the windward side.

### Relative air velocity

Because the wave surface is moving, the pressure force cannot be calculated using only the air velocity.

The relevant quantity is the velocity of the air relative to the wave:

```math
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{rel}}
=
\boldsymbol{u}
-
\boldsymbol{C}
```

where:

- $\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is the air velocity relative to the moving wave;
- $\boldsymbol{u}$ is the air velocity obtained from the LES;
- $\boldsymbol{C}$ is the local horizontal velocity of the wave surface.

In simple terms:

```math
\text{relative air velocity}
=
\text{air velocity}
-
\text{wave velocity}
```

If $\boldsymbol{u}$ is much larger than $\boldsymbol{C}$, the relative velocity is large and the pressure drag is strong.

If $\boldsymbol{u}$ and $\boldsymbol{C}$ are similar, the relative velocity is small and the pressure drag decreases.

If $\boldsymbol{C}$ becomes larger than $\boldsymbol{u}$, the relative airflow may reverse direction. In this case, the opposite wave slope may become the windward side.

### Direction of the wave slope

The horizontal direction of the local wave slope is described by:

```math
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
=
\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta}
{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|}
```

where:

- $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ is the horizontal unit vector normal to the local wave slope;
- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta$ gives the direction in which the water surface rises;
- $|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|$ gives the magnitude of the local wave slope.

Only the part of the relative airflow directed against the wave slope produces pressure drag.

This part is calculated using:

```math
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
```

where:

- $\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C}$ is the relative air velocity;
- $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ is the direction of the local wave slope;
- the dot represents the scalar product between the two vectors.

A large value means that the relative airflow moves strongly against the wave slope.

A value equal to zero means that the airflow moves parallel to the wave crest and does not directly encounter the slope.

### Local velocity of the wave surface

For a general moving water surface, the local horizontal wave velocity is calculated as:

```math
C_i
=
-
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2}
```

where:

- $C_i$ is the wave-surface velocity component in horizontal direction $i$;
- $\partial\eta/\partial t$ is the change in the surface elevation with time;
- $\partial\eta/\partial x_i$ is the wave slope in horizontal direction $i$;
- $|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2$ is the squared magnitude of the total horizontal gradient;
- $i=1$ and $i=2$ represent the two horizontal directions.

The equation combines the temporal change in the surface with its spatial slope to determine how rapidly the local wave shape moves horizontally.

For a simple sinusoidal wave:

```math
\eta(x,t)
=
a\cos(kx-\omega t)
```

where:

- $a$ is the wave amplitude;
- $k$ is the wavenumber;
- $\omega$ is the angular frequency;
- $x$ is the horizontal position;
- $t$ is time.

The wave velocity then becomes:

```math
C
=
\frac{\omega}{k}
```

where $C$ is the phase velocity of the wave.

The general expression can also be used for more complicated water surfaces containing waves with different wavelengths and propagation directions.

### Potential-flow approximation

The airflow over each local inclined surface is approximated as **potential flow**.

Potential flow assumes that the airflow is:

- inviscid, meaning that viscosity is neglected;
- irrotational, meaning that local rotation of the airflow is neglected.

The real airflow above water waves is turbulent and viscous. Therefore, potential flow does not describe the complete airflow.

In MOSD, it is only used to estimate the pressure produced when the large wave deflects the airflow.

The effects of friction and small unresolved waves are included separately through the Equilibrium Wall Model.

### Pressure on the windward surface

Bernoulli’s equation is used to estimate the average pressure increase on the windward side:

```math
\frac{\Delta\overline{P}}{\rho}
=
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|
```

where:

- $\Delta\overline{P}$ is the average pressure increase on the local wave surface;
- $\rho$ is the air density;
- $\boldsymbol{u}$ is the air velocity;
- $\boldsymbol{C}$ is the local wave velocity;
- $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ is the direction of the local wave slope;
- $|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|$ is the local wave steepness;
- $1/\pi$ is obtained from the potential-flow derivation.

The equation shows that the pressure increases with the square of the relative velocity directed against the wave.

It also shows that a steeper wave produces a greater pressure increase.

The main relationship is:

```math
\text{wave pressure}
\sim
(\text{relative velocity})^2
\times
\text{wave slope}
```

### Final wave-pressure stress

The pressure force is converted into a horizontal surface stress:

```math
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
=
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2
H
\left[
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
\right]
\hat{n}_i
```

where:

- $\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}$ is the surface stress produced by the large resolved waves;
- $\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C}$ is the relative air velocity;
- $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ is the direction of the local wave slope;
- $|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2$ represents the effect of wave steepness;
- $H$ is a switch that activates the pressure force only on the windward side;
- $\hat{n}_i$ determines the horizontal direction of the stress;
- $1/\pi$ is a theoretical coefficient rather than an experimentally fitted parameter.

The switch function can be understood as:

```math
H
=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{windward side},\\
0, & \text{leeward side}.
\end{cases}
```

Therefore:

```math
\text{large-wave drag}
\sim
(\text{relative wind speed})^2
(\text{wave slope})^2
```

A steep wave encountering a fast relative airflow produces strong pressure drag.

A flat surface, or a wave moving at nearly the same velocity as the air, produces little pressure drag.

### Main assumptions

The Windward Potential Flow Model makes the following simplifications:

- each local part of the wave is approximated as an inclined plane;
- every computational cell is treated independently;
- potential flow is used to estimate the pressure;
- the local flow is assumed to be steady;
- the pressure force is included only on the windward side;
- the pressure force on the leeward side is neglected;
- the model is mainly intended for waves with relatively small slopes.

## 2.2 Discussion of the Windward Potential Flow Model

This section compares the Windward Potential Flow Model with an earlier model called the **Surface Gradient Drag model**, abbreviated as **SGD**.

### Difference between SGD and WPM

The Surface Gradient Drag model assumes that all the momentum carried by the incoming airflow is converted into drag when the air encounters a surface obstacle.

In simple terms, the SGD assumption is:

```math
\text{incoming momentum}
\longrightarrow
\text{entirely converted into drag}
```

This assumption can produce excessive drag, especially when the wave slope is small.

In reality, only part of the incoming airflow momentum is transferred to the wave. The remaining airflow continues moving over the surface and leaves the local region.

```math
\text{incoming momentum}
=
\text{momentum transferred to the wave}
+
\text{momentum leaving the region}
```

The Windward Potential Flow Model does not assume that all the incoming momentum becomes drag. Instead, it calculates the pressure distribution created when the airflow is deflected over the windward slope.

Therefore:

- SGD calculates drag directly from the incoming momentum;
- WPM calculates drag from the pressure acting on the wave surface.

### Direction of the force

In the SGD model, the direction of the drag is determined mainly by the direction of the incoming relative airflow.

In the WPM model, the direction of the pressure force is determined by the orientation of the wave surface:

```math
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
=
\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta}
{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|}
```

where:

- $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ is the horizontal direction of the local wave slope;
- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta$ is the gradient of the surface elevation;
- $|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|$ is the magnitude of the wave slope.

This is physically reasonable because a pressure force acts perpendicular to the local surface.

### Dependence on wave steepness

For a simple sinusoidal wave:

```math
\eta(x,t)
=
a\cos(kx-\omega t)
```

where:

- $\eta$ is the surface elevation;
- $a$ is the wave amplitude;
- $k$ is the wavenumber;
- $\omega$ is the angular frequency;
- $x$ is the horizontal position;
- $t$ is time.

The wave steepness is:

```math
ak
```

The mean pressure stress predicted by the WPM approximately follows:

```math
\overline{\tau}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
\approx
\frac{1}{4\pi}
(u-c)^2
(ak)^2
```

where:

- $\overline{\tau}^{\mathrm{WPM}}$ is the average pressure stress produced by the resolved wave;
- $u$ is the air velocity;
- $c$ is the wave phase velocity;
- $u-c$ is the relative air velocity;
- $a$ is the wave amplitude;
- $k$ is the wavenumber;
- $ak$ is the wave steepness.

This equation shows that the mean wave stress is proportional to the square of the relative velocity:

```math
\overline{\tau}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
\sim
(u-c)^2
```

It is also proportional to the square of the wave steepness:

```math
\overline{\tau}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
\sim
(ak)^2
```

Therefore:

```math
\text{larger relative velocity}
\longrightarrow
\text{larger wave stress}
```

```math
\text{steeper wave}
\longrightarrow
\text{larger wave stress}
```

This means that a small increase in wave steepness can produce a relatively large increase in pressure drag because the steepness appears squared.

### Main result of Section 2.2

The WPM is more physically specific than the SGD model because it estimates the pressure acting on the wave instead of assuming that all the incoming air momentum becomes drag.

The main WPM relationship is:

```math
\text{wave-pressure stress}
\sim
(\text{relative velocity})^2
(\text{wave steepness})^2
```

## 2.3 Equilibrium Model for Fully Unresolved Surface Roughness

The Windward Potential Flow Model represents the pressure drag caused by the large waves included in the LES.

However, the water surface can also contain much smaller features, such as:

- small ripples;
- capillary waves;
- small wind-generated waves;
- viscous surface effects.

These features may be smaller than the LES grid and therefore cannot be represented individually.

The **Equilibrium Wall Model**, abbreviated as **EQM**, represents their combined effect as surface friction.

```math
\text{large resolved waves}
\longrightarrow
\text{WPM pressure drag}
```

```math
\text{small unresolved waves}
\longrightarrow
\text{EQM friction}
```

### Equilibrium wall stress

The equilibrium stress is calculated as:

```math
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{EQM}}
=
\frac{1}{2}
c_f^{\mathrm{wm}}
\left(
Re_{\Delta},
\frac{z_0}{\Delta}
\right)
\hat{U}_{\mathrm{LES}}^{\Delta}
\hat{u}_i^{\Delta}
```

where:

- $\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{EQM}}$ is the equilibrium surface stress in horizontal direction $i$;
- $c_f^{\mathrm{wm}}$ is the wall-model friction factor;
- $Re_{\Delta}$ is the Reynolds number evaluated at the wall-model height;
- $z_0$ is the effective surface roughness length;
- $\Delta$ is the distance between the surface and the point where the LES velocity is taken;
- $\hat{u}_i^{\Delta}$ is the air-velocity component relative to the moving water surface;
- $\hat{U}_{\mathrm{LES}}^{\Delta}$ is the magnitude of the relative horizontal velocity;
- $i=1$ and $i=2$ represent the two horizontal directions.

The magnitude of the relative velocity is:

```math
\hat{U}_{\mathrm{LES}}^{\Delta}
=
\left|
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\Delta}
\right|
```

where $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\Delta}$ is the relative horizontal air-velocity vector at height $\Delta$.

The stress is therefore proportional to the square of the relative velocity because it contains the velocity magnitude multiplied by one velocity component.

In simplified form:

```math
\text{frictional stress}
\sim
\text{friction factor}
\times
(\text{relative velocity})^2
```

### Wave orbital velocity

Water particles move as a wave passes. This movement produces a local orbital velocity at the water surface.

If this movement is included, the relative velocity used by the Equilibrium Wall Model is:

```math
\hat{u}_i
=
u_i-u_{o,i}
```

where:

- $\hat{u}_i$ is the air velocity relative to the local water motion;
- $u_i$ is the LES air-velocity component;
- $u_{o,i}$ is the horizontal orbital-velocity component of the water surface;
- $i$ identifies the horizontal direction.

This correction is important because surface friction depends on the velocity difference between the air and the moving water, not only on the absolute air velocity.

### Reynolds number

The Reynolds number used by the wall model is:

```math
Re_{\Delta}
=
\frac{
U_{\mathrm{LES}}^{\Delta}\Delta
}{
\nu
}
```

where:

- $Re_{\Delta}$ is the Reynolds number at the wall-model height;
- $U_{\mathrm{LES}}^{\Delta}$ is the horizontal air-speed magnitude at height $\Delta$;
- $\Delta$ is the wall-model height;
- $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity of air.

The Reynolds number compares inertial effects with viscous effects.

A low value indicates that viscosity has a relatively important influence. A high value indicates that inertial and turbulent effects dominate.

### Effective roughness length

The small unresolved surface features are represented using the effective roughness length:

```math
z_0
```

The roughness length is not necessarily the physical height of a particular ripple. It is a model parameter that represents the combined influence of all unresolved surface features on the airflow.

The dimensionless surface roughness is:

```math
\frac{z_0}{\Delta}
```

where:

- $z_0$ is the effective roughness length;
- $\Delta$ is the wall-model height.

A larger value indicates that unresolved surface features produce a stronger resistance to the airflow.

### Friction factor

The wall-model friction factor is:

```math
c_f^{\mathrm{wm}}
=
2
\left[
\left(
\frac{
Re_{\tau\Delta}^{\mathrm{s}}
}{
Re_{\Delta}
}
\right)^6
+
\left(
\frac{1}{\kappa}
\ln
\frac{\Delta}{z_0}
\right)^{-6}
\right]^{1/3}
```

where:

- $c_f^{\mathrm{wm}}$ is the wall-model friction factor;
- $Re_{\Delta}$ is the Reynolds number at the wall-model height;
- $Re_{\tau\Delta}^{\mathrm{s}}$ is the friction Reynolds number for a smooth surface;
- $\kappa$ is the von Kármán constant;
- $\Delta$ is the wall-model height;
- $z_0$ is the effective roughness length.

The von Kármán constant is approximately:

```math
\kappa=0.4
```

The formula combines two effects:

1. friction produced by viscosity over a smooth surface;
2. friction produced by unresolved surface roughness.

For a nearly smooth surface, the smooth-surface contribution dominates.

For a strongly rough surface, the roughness contribution dominates.

For an intermediate surface, both contributions are important.

```math
\text{smooth surface}
\longrightarrow
\text{viscous friction dominates}
```

```math
\text{rough surface}
\longrightarrow
\text{roughness drag dominates}
```

This allows the same model to represent smooth, transitionally rough and fully rough surfaces.

### Representation of small ripples

Small ripples and capillary waves can increase the resistance experienced by the airflow.

MOSD does not calculate the position and movement of every ripple. Instead, their average effect is represented through the roughness length.

The characteristic ripple height is described by:

```math
\eta_{\mathrm{rms}}'
```

where $\eta_{\mathrm{rms}}'$ is the root-mean-square height of the unresolved ripples.

The corresponding effective roughness length is:

```math
z_0
=
\eta_{\mathrm{rms}}'
e^{-8.5\kappa}
```

where:

- $z_0$ is the effective roughness length;
- $\eta_{\mathrm{rms}}'$ is the root-mean-square ripple height;
- $\kappa$ is the von Kármán constant;
- $e$ is the exponential constant.

This equation converts the measured or prescribed ripple height into a roughness length that can be used by the Equilibrium Wall Model.

Therefore, the small ripples are not represented individually, but their average influence on the airflow is retained.

### Main result of Section 2.3

The Equilibrium Wall Model represents two effects:

```math
\text{EQM stress}
=
\text{viscous friction}
+
\text{unresolved roughness effect}
```

It can therefore be used for:

- a smooth water surface;
- a surface covered by small unresolved ripples;
- a surface in the transition between smooth and rough conditions.

## 2.4 Summary of the Moving Surface Drag Model

The complete MOSD model combines the Windward Potential Flow Model with the Equilibrium Wall Model:

```math
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{MOSD}}
=
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
+
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{EQM}}
```

The complete stress equation is:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{MOSD}}
={}&
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2
\hat{n}_i
H
\left[
(u_j-C_j)
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_j}
\right]
\\
&+
\frac{1}{2}
c_f^{\mathrm{wm}}
\hat{u}_i^{\Delta}
\hat{U}_{\mathrm{LES}}^{\Delta},
\qquad i=1,2.
\end{aligned}
```

The first part is the WPM contribution:

```math
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2
\hat{n}_i
H
\left[
(u_j-C_j)
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_j}
\right]
```

It represents the pressure drag produced by the windward sides of the large resolved waves.

The second part is the EQM contribution:

```math
\frac{1}{2}
c_f^{\mathrm{wm}}
\hat{u}_i^{\Delta}
\hat{U}_{\mathrm{LES}}^{\Delta}
```

It represents viscous friction and the average effect of small unresolved surface features.

### Symbols in the complete equation

- $\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{MOSD}}$ is the total MOSD surface stress;
- $\boldsymbol{u}$ is the LES air velocity used by the WPM;
- $\boldsymbol{C}$ is the local horizontal velocity of the wave surface;
- $\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C}$ is the air velocity relative to the wave;
- $\eta(x,y,t)$ is the prescribed water-surface elevation;
- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta$ is the horizontal surface gradient;
- $|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|$ is the local wave steepness;
- $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ is the horizontal unit vector in the direction of steepest surface rise;
- $\hat{n}_i$ is the component of this unit vector in direction $i$;
- $H$ is the switch that activates WPM pressure drag only on the windward side;
- $c_f^{\mathrm{wm}}$ is the wall-model friction factor;
- $\hat{u}_i^{\Delta}$ is the relative velocity component used by the EQM;
- $\hat{U}_{\mathrm{LES}}^{\Delta}$ is the magnitude of the relative velocity used by the EQM;
- $i$ identifies the direction of the calculated stress;
- $j$ is a repeated index indicating summation over the horizontal directions.

### Different regions where the two stresses act

The WPM pressure stress acts only on the windward side:

```math
\text{WPM}
\longrightarrow
\text{windward side only}
```

The EQM frictional stress acts over the entire water surface:

```math
\text{EQM}
\longrightarrow
\text{windward and leeward sides}
```

The EQM also acts on the leeward side because viscous friction and small-scale roughness can still produce tangential stress even when the large-scale pressure force is neglected.

### Information required by the model

The WPM requires:

- the LES air velocity;
- the surface elevation as a function of position and time;
- the local wave slope;
- the local wave velocity.

The EQM requires:

- the relative air velocity at the wall-model height;
- the wall-model height;
- the air viscosity;
- the effective roughness length.

The wave surface is prescribed as:

```math
\eta(x,y,t)
```

MOSD uses this information to calculate the effect of the moving surface on the airflow.

### One-way coupling

The model uses **one-way coupling**.

This means that the prescribed waves affect the airflow, but the calculated airflow does not modify the waves during the simulation.

```math
\text{prescribed waves}
\longrightarrow
\text{airflow}
```

The reverse interaction is not calculated:

```math
\text{airflow}
\mathrel{\not\longrightarrow}
\text{wave evolution}
```

Therefore, MOSD can calculate how a known moving wave field changes the wind, but it does not calculate wave growth, wave deformation or wave breaking caused by the wind.

### Applicability

The model can be applied to more than a single sinusoidal wave because it uses the local surface elevation:

```math
\eta(x,y,t)
```

In principle, the surface can contain:

- different wavelengths;
- different wave directions;
- waves moving at different velocities;
- irregular or multiscale wave fields.

The model only requires the position and time dependence of the prescribed water surface.

### Main limitations

The current MOSD formulation has several limitations:

- it is mainly intended for wave slopes smaller than approximately $0.4$;
- it assumes that the leeward pressure force is negligible;
- it does not calculate breaking waves;
- it does not calculate nonlinear interactions between waves;
- it does not calculate the evolution of the wave field;
- it treats the wave effect on the wind as one-way coupling;
- it approximates each wave section as an independent local ramp.

### Core concept

The complete MOSD model separates the surface stress according to the physical scale responsible for it:

```math
\boxed{
\text{MOSD}
=
\text{resolved-wave pressure drag}
+
\text{unresolved-scale friction}
}
```

The WPM retains information about the position and movement of the large waves.

The EQM represents the combined average effect of small ripples, roughness and viscous friction.

This allows phase-dependent wind–wave effects to be included in LES without directly resolving the complete airflow around the moving water surface.



## 2.1 Derivation of the wave-induced pressure model

The model considers a small part of the wave surface as a moving inclined plane. The airflow approaches this surface with a velocity relative to the moving wave.

The main assumptions are:

- the local wave slope is small;
- the airflow is incompressible and approximately inviscid near the surface;
- the windward side produces a pressure force;
- the pressure force on the leeward side is neglected;
- each surface-grid cell is treated independently.

### Main symbols

- $\eta(x_1,x_2,t)$ is the surface elevation.
- $\boldsymbol{u}=(u_1,u_2)$ is the horizontal air velocity.
- $\boldsymbol{C}=(C_1,C_2)$ is the local horizontal velocity of the wave surface.
- $\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C}$ is the air velocity relative to the moving wave.
- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta$ is the horizontal surface gradient.
- $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ is the horizontal unit vector pointing in the direction of maximum surface rise.
- $\rho$ is the air density.
- $\Delta P$ is the pressure increase produced by the wave.
- $\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}$ is the wave-induced surface stress.

The horizontal surface gradient is:

```math
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
=
\left(
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_1},
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_2}
\right)
```

Its magnitude is:

```math
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|
=
\sqrt{
\left(\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_1}\right)^2
+
\left(\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_2}\right)^2
}
```

The horizontal unit vector in the direction of the slope is:

```math
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
=
\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta}
{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|}
```

This is not the complete three-dimensional normal to the water surface. It is the horizontal direction in which the surface elevation increases most rapidly.

---

### Flow over a moving inclined surface

First, the model considers a two-dimensional inclined surface moving with velocity $c$.

The relative air velocity is:

```math
u-c
```

If $u>c$, the airflow moves towards and over the wave. If $u=c$, there is no relative horizontal motion between the air and the wave.

The airflow around the inclined surface is described using the streamfunction:

```math
\psi(r,\theta)
=
A r^n
\sin\left[n(\theta-\alpha)\right]
```

where:

- $\psi$ is the streamfunction;
- $r$ is the distance from the stagnation point;
- $\theta$ is the polar angle;
- $\alpha$ is the angle of the inclined surface;
- $A$ is a constant;
- $n$ is determined by the surface angle.

The exponent $n$ is:

```math
n
=
\frac{\pi}{\pi-\alpha}
```

This equation describes the idealized airflow around the inclined surface. The surface angle $\alpha$ controls how strongly the airflow is deflected.

The velocity normal to the inclined surface is zero:

```math
V_\theta=0
```

This means that the airflow cannot pass through the surface.

The velocity parallel to the surface is obtained from the streamfunction:

```math
V_r
=
-\frac{1}{r}
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta}
```

At the outer edge of the local surface element, the parallel velocity must equal the relative air–wave velocity:

```math
V_r(r=\Delta x)=u-c
```

Here, $\Delta x$ is the horizontal size of the local surface element.

Using this boundary condition gives:

```math
V_r
=
(u-c)
\left(
\frac{r}{\Delta x}
\right)^{n-1}
```

At the stagnation point, where $r=0$, the velocity is zero. At $r=\Delta x$, the velocity reaches $u-c$.

---

### Pressure over the inclined surface

Bernoulli’s equation relates the local airflow velocity to the pressure:

```math
\frac{\Delta P(r)}{\rho}
=
\frac{1}{2}(u-c)^2
\left[
1-
\left(
\frac{r}{\Delta x}
\right)^{
\frac{2\alpha}{\pi-\alpha}
}
\right]
```

The pressure is highest near the stagnation point because the airflow slows down there.

At $r=0$:

```math
\frac{\Delta P(0)}{\rho}
=
\frac{1}{2}(u-c)^2
```

At $r=\Delta x$:

```math
\Delta P(\Delta x)=0
```

Therefore, the pressure decreases from the stagnation point towards the outer edge of the local inclined surface.

The mean pressure over the surface element is:

```math
\frac{\Delta\overline{P}}{\rho}
=
\frac{1}{2}(u-c)^2
\frac{1}{\Delta x}
\int_0^{\Delta x}
\left[
1-
\left(
\frac{r}{\Delta x}
\right)^{
\frac{2\alpha}{\pi-\alpha}
}
\right]
\,dr
```

Evaluating the integral gives:

```math
\frac{\Delta\overline{P}}{\rho}
=
(u-c)^2
\frac{\alpha}{\pi+\alpha}
```

This equation shows that the mean pressure increases when:

- the relative air–wave velocity $u-c$ increases;
- the surface angle $\alpha$ increases.

For a small surface angle:

```math
\alpha\ll\pi
```

the denominator can be approximated as:

```math
\pi+\alpha\approx\pi
```

Therefore:

```math
\frac{\Delta\overline{P}}{\rho}
\approx
\frac{1}{\pi}
(u-c)^2\alpha
```

This is the small-slope form used to construct the three-dimensional model.

---

### Extension to a general wave surface

For a general surface, the small angle $\alpha$ is approximated by the magnitude of the surface gradient:

```math
\alpha
\approx
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|
```

The relevant relative velocity is the part of $\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C}$ directed along the surface slope:

```math
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
```

The mean pressure becomes:

```math
\frac{\Delta\overline{P}}{\rho}
=
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|
```

The dot product selects only the component of the relative airflow that moves towards or away from the inclined surface.

For example, if the airflow moves perpendicular to the slope direction:

```math
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
=
0
```

the model predicts no wave-induced pressure.

---

### Local velocity of the moving wave surface

The local horizontal wave velocity is initially assumed to be parallel to the surface gradient:

```math
C_i
=
\zeta
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i},
\qquad i=1,2
```

Here, $\zeta$ is an unknown coefficient that determines the magnitude and sign of the local wave velocity.

A point moving with the same surface elevation satisfies:

```math
\delta\eta
=
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
\delta x_i
+
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}
\delta t
=
0
```

Because:

```math
C_i
=
\frac{\delta x_i}{\delta t}
```

the constant-elevation condition becomes:

```math
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}
+
C_i
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
=
0
```

Substituting the assumed expression for $C_i$ gives:

```math
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}
+
\zeta
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
=
0
```

Using:

```math
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
=
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2
```

gives:

```math
\zeta
=
-
\frac{
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}
}{
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2
}
```

Therefore, the local wave velocity is:

```math
C_i
=
-
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2},
\qquad i=1,2
```

This equation calculates the local horizontal movement of the surface shape directly from the surface elevation $\eta(x_1,x_2,t)$.

For a simple travelling wave:

```math
\eta
=
a\cos(kx-\omega t)
```

the phase velocity is:

```math
c
=
\frac{\omega}{k}
```

and the local-velocity equation gives:

```math
C_1=c
```

Thus, for a regular travelling wave, the calculated local surface velocity agrees with the usual wave phase velocity.

---

### Pressure force on a surface element

The horizontal pressure force is:

```math
\boldsymbol{F}
=
-
\int_A
\Delta P
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\,dA
```

If the mean pressure is used over one small surface element:

```math
\boldsymbol{F}
=
-
\Delta\overline{P}
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\Delta A
```

The negative sign means that the force exerted by the surface on the air acts opposite to $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$.

The elevation change across a small horizontal displacement is:

```math
\Delta\eta
=
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
\Delta x_i
```

For a square horizontal grid cell:

```math
(\Delta L)^2
=
\Delta x_1\Delta x_2
```

The elevation change in the slope direction is approximated as:

```math
\Delta\eta
=
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|
\Delta L
```

The projected area on which the pressure acts is:

```math
\Delta A
=
\Delta\eta\,\Delta L
```

Therefore:

```math
\Delta A
=
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|
(\Delta L)^2
```

Substituting the pressure model and projected area gives the horizontal force component:

```math
f_i
=
-
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|
\hat n_i
\left(
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|\Delta L
\right)
\Delta L
```

Equivalently:

```math
f_i
=
-
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2
\hat n_i
(\Delta L)^2
```

This force increases with:

- the square of the relative velocity directed into the wave;
- the square of the local wave slope;
- the horizontal area of the grid cell.

---

### Wave-induced surface stress

The surface stress is the force divided by the horizontal grid-cell area:

```math
\tau_{i3}
=
-\frac{f_i}{(\Delta L)^2}
```

Substituting the force gives:

```math
\tau_{i3}
=
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2
\hat n_i
```

The subscript $i$ identifies the horizontal force direction. The index $3$ identifies the vertical surface across which horizontal momentum is transferred.

---

### Windward-side condition

The model applies the pressure force only when the relative airflow moves into the rising side of the wave.

Define:

```math
s
=
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
```

If:

```math
s>0
```

the relative airflow moves towards increasing surface elevation. This is the windward side, so the pressure force is included.

If:

```math
s<0
```

the relative airflow moves towards decreasing surface elevation. This is the leeward side, so the pressure force is neglected.

This condition is introduced using a Heaviside function:

```math
H_+(s)
=
\begin{cases}
1, & s>0,\\
0, & s<0.
\end{cases}
```

For $s\neq0$, it can be written as:

```math
H_+(s)
=
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
1+\frac{s}{|s|}
\right)
```

The paper prints the expression:

```math
H(s)
=
\frac{1}{2}
\frac{s-|s|}{s}
```

However, this printed expression equals zero when $s>0$ and one when $s<0$, which is opposite to the windward-side condition described in the text. The positive-part definition $H_+(s)$ above is consistent with the stated physical meaning.

---

### Final wave-induced pressure stress

The final stress produced by the resolved moving wave is:

```math
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
=
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2
H_+
\left[
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
\right]
\hat n_i,
\qquad i=1,2
```

Each part has a specific meaning:

- $\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C}$ is the airflow velocity relative to the moving wave.
- $(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ is the relative velocity directed along the local slope.
- The square of this velocity represents the dynamic-pressure effect.
- $|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2$ represents the influence of the wave slope.
- $H_+$ applies the force only on the windward side.
- $\hat n_i$ gives the horizontal direction of the stress.
- $1/\pi$ comes from the inclined-surface pressure derivation.

For a locally flat surface:

```math
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|=0
```

and therefore:

```math
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}=0
```

Thus, a flat surface produces no resolved-wave form drag in this model.

The model is mainly intended for waves with relatively small local slopes. It also assumes that the air velocity used in the stress equation represents the airflow outside the immediate pressure-disturbance region near the surface.
