## 2. Moving Surface Drag (MOSD) model

The **Moving Surface Drag model (MOSD)** is a wall model used to represent the effect of moving water waves on the airflow in a Large-Eddy Simulation (LES).

In LES, the computational grid is usually too coarse to reproduce all the details of the airflow immediately above the water surface. MOSD therefore calculates the effect of the waves and applies it to the airflow as a surface stress.

The total MOSD stress contains two parts:

```math
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{MOSD}}
=
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
+
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{EQM}}
```

where:

- `WPM` is the **Windward Potential Flow Model**;
- `EQM` is the **Equilibrium Wall Model**.

The two models represent different wave scales:

- the WPM calculates the pressure drag caused by the large waves represented by the LES;
- the EQM represents the combined effect of small unresolved waves, surface roughness and viscous friction.

Therefore:

```math
\text{total surface stress}
=
\text{large-wave pressure drag}
+
\text{small-scale friction}
```

The large waves are described by their surface elevation:

```math
\eta(x,y,t)
```

This function gives the height of the water surface at each horizontal position and time.

The wave is **horizontally resolved** because the LES knows its position, slope and movement. However, it is **vertically unresolved** because the computational mesh does not follow the complete curved shape of the wave.

The LES can therefore use a flat lower boundary while MOSD introduces the effect of the moving wave through the surface stress.

This makes MOSD a middle ground between:

- a simple roughness model, which does not retain information about the position of the waves;
- a fully wave-resolved simulation, which requires a computational grid that follows the water surface.

## 2.1 Windward Potential Flow Model

The **Windward Potential Flow Model (WPM)** calculates the pressure drag produced by the large resolved waves.

The central idea is simple: when the wind encounters the front of a wave, the airflow is forced upward. This change in direction produces increased pressure on the wave surface.

The pressure force acts on both the wave and the air:

- the airflow transfers momentum to the wave;
- the wave exerts an opposite force on the airflow.

This exchange of momentum appears in the LES as a surface stress.

### Local representation of the wave

MOSD divides the wave surface into small sections corresponding to the LES grid cells.

Inside each cell, the curved wave surface is approximated as a small inclined plane, similar to a ramp.

The inclination of this ramp is determined by the local gradient:

```math
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
=
\left(
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x},
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}
\right)
```

The magnitude of the gradient represents the local wave slope:

```math
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|
```

Therefore:

```math
\text{large slope}
\longrightarrow
\text{stronger airflow deflection}
\longrightarrow
\text{larger pressure force}
```

For a locally flat surface:

```math
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta=0
```

there is no pressure drag from the large wave.

This does not mean that the total surface stress is zero. Friction and the effects of small unresolved waves are still represented by the equilibrium wall model.

### Windward and leeward sides

The model distinguishes between the two sides of the wave.

The **windward side** is the side into which the relative airflow is moving. The wind encounters this surface and is forced upward, producing increased pressure.

The **leeward side** is the side from which the relative airflow moves away. On this side, the airflow may separate from the surface and form a sheltered region.

MOSD makes the simplifying assumption that the pressure force on the leeward side is negligible.

Therefore:

```math
\text{windward side}
\longrightarrow
\text{pressure drag included}
```

```math
\text{leeward side}
\longrightarrow
\text{pressure drag neglected}
```

This assumption is necessary because applying ideal potential flow to both sides of the wave would produce pressure forces that cancel each other. The average form drag would then be zero, which would not correctly represent the momentum transfer observed over real waves.

### Relative wind–wave velocity

The pressure force depends on the velocity of the air relative to the moving wave.

The relevant velocity is:

```math
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{rel}}
=
\boldsymbol{u}
-
\boldsymbol{C}
```

where:

- \(\boldsymbol{u}\) is the air velocity obtained from the LES;
- \(\boldsymbol{C}\) is the local horizontal velocity of the wave surface;
- \(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{rel}}\) is the velocity of the airflow as observed from the moving wave.

The wave velocity must be subtracted because the wave surface is not stationary.

For example:

- if the wind is much faster than the wave, the relative velocity is large and the pressure drag is strong;
- if the wind and wave move at similar velocities, the relative velocity is small and the pressure drag decreases;
- if the wave moves faster than the wind, the side experiencing the pressure force can change.

Therefore, the **windward side is determined by the relative airflow**, not only by the absolute wind direction.

### Direction of the local wave slope

The horizontal unit vector normal to the local wave surface is:

```math
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
=
\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta}
{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|}
```

This vector identifies the horizontal direction in which the wave surface rises most rapidly.

Only the part of the relative airflow directed against the slope produces pressure drag. This part is found using the scalar product:

```math
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
```

If this value is large, the airflow is moving strongly against the wave surface.

If it is zero, the airflow is moving parallel to the wave crest and does not directly encounter the slope.

### Local velocity of the wave surface

For a general moving surface, MOSD calculates the local wave velocity using:

```math
C_i
=
-
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_i}
\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2}
```

This expression combines:

- the temporal change in surface elevation;
- the local spatial slope.

It determines how rapidly the surface shape is moving horizontally in each direction.

For a simple sinusoidal wave:

```math
\eta(x,t)
=
a\cos(kx-\omega t)
```

the local surface velocity becomes the usual wave phase velocity:

```math
C
=
\frac{\omega}{k}
```

where:

- \(a\) is the wave amplitude;
- \(k\) is the wavenumber;
- \(\omega\) is the angular frequency.

The general expression can also be applied to more complicated surfaces containing waves travelling in different directions.

### Pressure on the windward surface

The airflow over each local ramp is approximated as **potential flow**.

Potential flow assumes that the airflow is:

- inviscid, meaning that viscosity is neglected;
- irrotational, meaning that the local rotation of the flow is neglected.

This is not a complete description of the real turbulent airflow. It is used only to obtain a simple estimate of the pressure force produced by the large wave geometry.

Using Bernoulli’s equation, the average pressure increase on the local wave surface is approximated by:

```math
\frac{\Delta\overline{P}}{\rho}
=
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|
```

where:

- \(\Delta\overline{P}\) is the average pressure increase;
- \(\rho\) is the air density;
- \(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C}\) is the relative velocity;
- \(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\) identifies the direction of the slope;
- \(|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|\) is the magnitude of the slope.

The equation shows that pressure increases with:

- the square of the velocity directed against the wave;
- the local wave slope.

In simple terms:

```math
\text{wave pressure}
\sim
(\text{relative velocity})^2
\times
\text{wave slope}
```

### Final WPM stress

The pressure force is converted into a horizontal surface stress:

```math
\tau_{i3}^{\mathrm{WPM}}
=
\frac{1}{\pi}
\left|
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
\right|^2
|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2
H\left[
(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C})
\cdot
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta
\right]
\hat{n}_i
```

The different terms have the following meanings:

- \(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{C}\) gives the relative wind–wave velocity;
- \(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\) gives the direction of the local slope;
- \(|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta|^2\) represents the influence of wave steepness;
- \(H\) activates the force only on the windward side;
- \(\hat{n}_i\) gives the direction of the resulting stress;
- \(1/\pi\) is obtained from the potential-flow derivation and is not an empirical fitting coefficient.

The function \(H\) acts like a switch:

```math
H
=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{windward side},\\
0, & \text{leeward side}.
\end{cases}
```

The main physical relationship is therefore:

```math
\text{large-wave drag}
\sim
(\text{relative wind speed})^2
(\text{wave slope})^2
```

A wave produces stronger pressure drag when it is steeper and when the airflow encounters it with a larger relative velocity.

### Main assumptions

The WPM is based on several simplifications:

- each part of the wave is approximated as a small inclined plane;
- each LES cell is treated independently;
- the airflow used to calculate pressure is ideal potential flow;
- the flow is assumed to be locally steady;
- the pressure force is applied only on the windward side;
- the leeward pressure force is neglected;
- the approximation is mainly intended for relatively small wave slopes.

These assumptions reduce the computational cost because the detailed airflow close to the moving water surface does not need to be directly resolved.

The WPM therefore provides the **phase-dependent pressure drag of the large waves**, while the equilibrium wall model provides the **average frictional effect of the unresolved small waves**.
